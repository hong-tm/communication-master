*   What is a parasocial interaction?  

Parasocial interaction (PSI) is a psychological semblance of interpersonal exchange where audience members develop a sense of knowing a performer in mass media. PSIs are similar to face-to-face communication and may lead to a parasocial relationship, which is a one-sided sense of connection with the performer.

American sociologists Donald Horton and R. Richard Wohl coined the term PSI in 1956. PSIs occur when audience members feel they are actively interacting with a media personality. Human brains appear to process PSIs like real-life interactions because of the novelty of technologically mediated encounters. PSIs are strongest when a media personality cultivates the illusion of interpersonal intimacy.

*   How do parasocial interactions differ from regular friendships?  

Parasocial interactions (PSIs) differ from regular friendships because they are one-sided relationships where an audience member feels a connection to a media figure. Unlike mutual friendships, PSIs lack reciprocal exchange and are mediated through mass media.

Human brains process PSIs similarly to real-life interactions, but they occur with media personalities who cultivate intimacy, creating a sense of closeness with their audience. These interactions can lead to parasocial relationships characterized by comfort and commitment, and are common in children and adolescents. The rise of social media has intensified PSIs, sometimes blurring the lines between interpersonal and parasocial connections.

*   What are some effects of parasocial interactions on people's behavior or feelings?  

Parasocial interactions (PSIs) create a sense of interpersonal exchange where audience members feel they know a media performer, producing psychological effects similar to face-to-face communication. As PSIs grow, audience members may develop parasocial relationships, characterized by comfort and commitment. When a show ends, committed viewers may feel distress, known as a "parasocial breakup".

PSIs are common among children because their ability to distinguish real from artificial is less developed than that of adults, which allows parasocial relationships to form more easily. Digital PSIs can also lead to "parasocial comparison," where viewers feel inferior to on-screen personas, which can cause poor body image. During periods of isolation, such as the COVID-19 pandemic, parasocial relationships can increase as a coping mechanism.

**parasocial interaction (PSI)**, semblance of interpersonal exchange whereby members of an [audience](https://www.britannica.com/topic/audience-communications) come to feel that they personally know a [performer](https://www.britannica.com/art/performance-arts) they have encountered in [mass media](https://www.britannica.com/topic/mass-media). Parasocial interactions (PSIs) are thought to have a psychological effect similar to that of face-to-face communication. Over time, PSIs with a performer may lead audience members to develop a parasocial relationship—a one-sided sense of connection with the performer.

The term _parasocial interaction_ first appeared in the writings of American sociologists Donald Horton and R. Richard Wohl in the 1956 article “Mass Communication and Para-Social Interaction: Observations on Intimacy at a Distance.” The article describes how PSIs may gradually lead to the formation of a parasocial relationship. Most theoretical work attempting to define and differentiate PSIs and parasocial relationships was published in the latter half of the 20th century. Generally, modern sociologists and media theorists agree that the concepts are distinct but deeply related. The Parasocial Interaction Scale, devised in the 1980s in order to better quantify PSIs and parasocial relationships, asks subjects to answer questions about both phenomena.

PSIs occur when audience members feel that they are actively interacting with a mass media personality. Human brains appear to process PSIs in much the same way as real-life interpersonal interactions because of the novelty of technologically mediated encounters. While people do recognize the artificiality of the media apparatus, their perception of PSIs causes a real psychological reaction.

PSIs are strongest when a media personality cultivates the illusion of interpersonal [intimacy](https://www.britannica.com/topic/intimacy). Certain genres, programs, and celebrities have purposely fostered such a sense of intimacy in their tone and setting. For example, TV [talk shows](https://www.britannica.com/topic/talk-show) have their hosts directly address the camera as if in conversation with each viewer, creating the illusion of face-to-face closeness. [Situation comedies](https://www.britannica.com/art/situation-comedy) manufacture familial settings that viewers grow more and more accustomed to. Certain podcasts and radio shows—especially those crafted around one or more hosts—adopt an informal tone resembling that of a gathering of friends.

As PSIs become increasingly frequent, many audience members enter into a parasocial relationship built on comfort, satisfaction, and commitment. In contrast, Horton and Wohl posited, people whose encounters with mass media figures are infrequent may feel detached and even cynical when they do encounter those figures. Indeed, the researchers suggested, audience members must tune in regularly and of their own volition for the relationship to become parasocial. Such relationships bridge [genre](https://www.britannica.com/art/genre-literature) and style. In one key study, researchers found that commitment levels (measured on a scale used for interpersonal relationships) for viewers of both fictional and nonfictional television programs were predicted by how invested the viewers were. Consequently, when a program went off the air, committed viewers experienced higher levels of distress, dubbed a “parasocial breakup,” than uncommitted viewers. Audience members often have a parasocial relationship with the same celebrity without feeling jealous of one another; in fact, in many cases, sharing their dedication to a mass media persona brings people closer together.

PSIs are especially common among children and adolescents. Young people’s ability to distinguish between the real and the artificial is less developed than that of adults, allowing parasocial relationships to form without adult levels of self-consciousness. Some children’s television programs use the parasocial space for education, having on-screen characters pause to invite viewers’ “participation.” PSIs help develop children’s ability to personify, thus opening the door for them to feel identification and [empathy](https://www.britannica.com/science/empathy).

In the digital age, PSIs have become increasingly common. [Social media](https://www.britannica.com/topic/social-media) platforms like [Instagram](https://www.britannica.com/money/Instagram), [YouTube](https://www.britannica.com/topic/YouTube), and [TikTok](https://www.britannica.com/topic/TikTok) can effectively “read” a user’s interests and suggest related content. This can lead to the formation of increasingly deep and closely held PSIs. The availability of these parasocial spaces, heightened by advancements in [smartphone](https://www.britannica.com/technology/smartphone) technology and smartphones’ increasing presence, can blur the line between interpersonal and parasocial interaction. Digital PSIs have also fostered a phenomenon called “parasocial comparison,” wherein viewers feel that they are inferior to an on-screen persona. This often manifests in viewers as poor body image. During the [COVID-19](https://www.britannica.com/science/COVID-19) pandemic, the quarantined world saw an uptick in parasocial relationships to cope with [isolation](https://www.britannica.com/science/isolation-preventive-medicine), the effects of which continue to be investigated.

*   What is social media?  

Social media includes platforms and technologies that allow people to communicate in one-to-one, one-to-many, and many-to-many exchanges. Social media allows people to simultaneously create, distribute, and consume content.

Social media helps people maintain connections across different geographic locations and can create new communities, offer social support, and reduce barriers to creating friendships. Social media also allows activists to fundraise, partner with influencers, promote events, and spread awareness of issues, which has fueled political protests.

However, social media also poses risks, such as digital addiction, cyberbullying, and privacy concerns. Social media companies harvest sensitive data that can be used to influence opinions, spread misinformation, and discriminate against vulnerable people.

*   How does social media spread news and information?  

Social media platforms have amplified the speed and reach of news and information dissemination. Unlike traditional media, social platforms allow anyone with a device to spread news, regardless of source credibility.

However, this hyper-connected environment can make it difficult to verify information, contributing to the spread of misinformation. Social media algorithms and echo chambers can further exacerbate the problem by prioritizing engagement over accuracy, and by creating filter bubbles where users are primarily exposed to information confirming existing beliefs. This can lead to the rapid spread of dangerous fads and inaccurate information, with severe consequences for public health and safety.

*   What are some benefits and risks of using social media?  

Social media offers numerous benefits, such as promoting community and enabling connections with friends and family across geographical boundaries. It provides access to supportive networks, especially beneficial for those who are isolated or have specific needs. Social media also serves as a vital tool for publicizing events and safety concerns.

The earliest forms of social media appeared almost as soon as technology could support them. E-mail and chat programs debuted in the early 1970s, but persistent communities did not surface until the creation of the discussion group network [USENET](https://www.britannica.com/technology/USENET) in 1979. USENET allowed users to post and receive messages within subject areas called [newsgroups](https://www.britannica.com/technology/newsgroup). USENET and other discussion forums, such as privately hosted [bulletin board systems](https://www.britannica.com/technology/bulletin-board-system) (BBSs), enabled individuals to interact, but each was essentially a closed system. With the release in 1993 of the Mosaic web [browser](https://www.britannica.com/technology/browser), those systems were joined with an easy-to-use graphical interface. The architecture of the [World Wide Web](https://www.britannica.com/topic/World-Wide-Web) made it possible to navigate from one site to another with a click, and faster Internet connections allowed for more multimedia content than could be found in the text-heavy newsgroups.

The first companies to create social networks based on web technology were [Classmates.com](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Classmatescom) and SixDegrees.com. Classmates.com, founded in 1995, used an aggressive pop-up advertising campaign to draw web surfers to its site. It based its social network on the existing connection between members of high-school and college graduating classes, armed service branches, and workplaces. [SixDegrees.com](https://www.britannica.com/topic/SixDegreescom) was the first true social networking site. It was launched in 1997 with most of the features that would come to characterize such sites: members could create profiles for themselves, maintain lists of friends, and contact one another through the site’s private messaging system. SixDegrees.com claimed to have attracted more than three million users by 2000, but it failed to translate those numbers into revenue and collapsed with countless other dot-coms when the “[bubble](https://www.britannica.com/money/dot-com-bubble)” burst that year for shares of [e-commerce](https://www.britannica.com/money/e-commerce) companies.

Nevertheless, social media sites became popular in the early 21st century. Those two sites were eventually supplanted by [Facebook](https://www.britannica.com/money/Facebook), which became one of the world’s most popular social media sites with billions of users worldwide. Other forms of social media emerged for the sharing of specific types of content. For example, [YouTube](https://www.britannica.com/topic/YouTube) allows users to share videos, and [TikTok](https://www.britannica.com/topic/TikTok) is specifically designed for the sharing of short videos. [LinkedIn](https://www.britannica.com/money/LinkedIn) emphasizes a user’s professional connections, where users create pages similar in structure to [résumés](https://www.britannica.com/topic/overview-of-sample-resumes-and-suggestions-for-how-to-write-them-2184519).

Concerns over the possible [negative effects](https://www.britannica.com/science/Internet-addiction) of social media are also growing in tandem with the burgeoning technology. For example, some observers suggest that social media sites spur greater [schadenfreude](https://www.britannica.com/topic/schadenfreude)—the emotional experience of pleasure in response to another’s misfortune—perhaps as a result of the [dehumanization](https://www.britannica.com/topic/dehumanization) that occurs when interacting through screens on [computers](https://www.britannica.com/technology/computer) and [mobile devices](https://www.britannica.com/technology/mobile-telephone). Some studies also suggest a strong tie between heavy social media use and increased [depression](https://www.britannica.com/science/depression-psychology), [anxiety](https://www.britannica.com/science/anxiety), [loneliness](https://www.britannica.com/science/loneliness), [suicidal](https://www.britannica.com/topic/suicide) tendencies, and [feelings](https://www.britannica.com/science/mental-health) of inadequacy. During his second tenure as [U.S. surgeon general](https://www.britannica.com/topic/surgeon-general-of-the-United-States), [Vivek Murthy](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Vivek-Murthy) raised concerns about social media’s impact on children and in 2024 he suggested mandated warning labels on social media sites.
