A [genderless language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genderless_language "Genderless language") is a [natural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_language "Natural language") or [constructed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructed_language "Constructed language")[language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language "Language") that has no distinctions of [grammatical gender](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_gender "Grammatical gender")—that is, no categories requiring [morphological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morphology_(linguistics) "Morphology (linguistics)")[agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agreement_(linguistics) "Agreement (linguistics)") between nouns and associated pronouns, adjectives, articles, or verbs.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-braun1-1)

The notion of a genderless language is distinct from that of **gender neutrality** or [gender-neutral language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender-neutral_language "Gender-neutral language"), which is wording that does not presuppose a particular [natural gender](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_gender "Natural gender"). A discourse in a grammatically genderless language is not necessarily gender-neutral,[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-braun1-1) although genderless languages exclude many possibilities for reinforcement of [gender](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender "Gender")-related stereotypes, as they still include words with gender-specific meanings (such as "son" and "daughter"), and may include gender distinctions among pronouns (such as "he" and "she").[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-braun1-1)

## Indo-European languages

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Indo-European languages")]

### Armenian

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Armenian")]

In [Armenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_language "Armenian language"), neither pronouns nor nouns have grammatical gender. The third-person pronoun [նա](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%D5%B6%D5%A1#Armenian "wikt:նա") (na) means both he and she, and [նրանք](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%D5%B6%D6%80%D5%A1%D5%B6%D6%84#Armenian "wikt:նրանք") (nranq) is for they.[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-Nenejian-2)

### Bengali

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Bengali")]

Bengali has the gender neutral pronoun [সে](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E0%A6%B8%E0%A7%87#Bengali "wikt:সে"); for indicating both he and she. Bengali verbs do not change from the subject's gender. Some Bengali nouns have separate words for masculine and feminine genders, although in modern Bengali people are encouraged to use gender neutral (often masculine-only) word forms.

### English

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: English")]

Main article: [Gender neutrality in English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_English "Gender neutrality in English")

[English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language "English language") lacks grammatical gender,[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-3)[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-4)[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-5) but can be considered to have a _pronominal gender system_ with semantic gender represented in the [pronouns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pronoun "Pronoun"). This system of gender is quite minimal compared to languages with grammatical gender.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-audring-6)

Historically, "he" referred to a generic person whose gender is unspecified in formal language, but the gender-neutral [singular they](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_they "Singular they") has long[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-7)[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-Gardiner_2016-8)[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-9) been common in informal language, and is becoming increasingly so in formal language.[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-10) The use of the neuter pronoun "it" is most commonly used in reference to non-personified objects and animals rather than for people.[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-11)

### Persian

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Persian")]

[Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_language "Persian language") is a gender-free language. In contrast to most other [Indo-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-European_languages "Indo-European languages") languages, Persian is grammatically gender-neutral.[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-12) It does not distinguish between masculine, feminine or neuter genders.[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-13)[Arabic loanwords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_vocabulary#Arabic_influence "Persian vocabulary") with the feminine ending ‏[ـة](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%D8%A9#Arabic "wikt:ة")‎ reduce to a genderless Persian [ـه](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%D9%80%D9%87#Persian "wikt:ـه"), which is pronounced _-a_ in Arabic, as well as in [Early New Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_New_Persian "Early New Persian") (ENP), [Classical Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Persian "Classical Persian") and Modern Afghan Persian (i.e., [Dari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dari "Dari")) and _-e_ in Modern [Iranian Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_Persian "Iranian Persian"). Many borrowed Arabic feminine words retain their Arabic feminine plural form ‏[ـات](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%D8%A7%D8%AA#Arabic "wikt:ات")‎ (_-āt_), but Persian descriptive adjectives modifying them have no gender. Arabic adjectives also lose their gender in Persian.[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-14)

For example, the word [او](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%D8%A7%D9%88#Persian "wikt:او") (-ō) means "he or she" and does not determine the gender of the person. All of the grammatical rules in Persian are gender-free.

## Non-Indo-European languages

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Non-Indo-European languages")]

### Basque

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Basque")]

The [Basque language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basque_language "Basque language") is largely gender-free. Most nouns have no gender, though there are different words for females and males in some cases (_ama_, "mother"; _aita_, "father"; _guraso_, "parent"). Some words are differentiated according to gender, like in the [English language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language "English language") (_aktoresa_, "actress"; _aktore_, "actor"), but they are not the main rule.[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-FOOTNOTELaka199632%E2%80%93332.3_Gender-15) For animals, there are particles (_oil+o_, "hen"; _oil+ar_, "cock"; _hartz eme_, "female bear"; _hartz arra_, "male bear") or different words (_behi_, "cow"; _zezen_, "bull").

[![Image 7: An old woman in traditional headwear says Hi, aizan! to a teen-aged girl, who can't hear her because she wears earphones.](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/60/Hi_aizan.png/250px-Hi_aizan.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Hi_aizan.png)

2019 _[Argia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argia\_(magazine) "Argia (magazine)")_ magazine cover about the loss of _noka_ (feminine _hika_). _Hi, aizan!_ means "Thou [female], hear!".

While there are no gender-specific pronouns, in some dialects, [Basque verbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basque_verbs#Familiar_forms_and_allocutive_indices_(hika) "Basque verbs") can agree [allocutively](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allocutive_agreement "Allocutive agreement") with the gender in the intimate singular second person (This is a mark of solidarity, providing no information since the listeners already know their genders.): _hik dun_, "you (female) have it"; _hik duk_, "you (male) have it". The verb is marked for addressee's gender, if they are intimate singular, whether or not they are referred to in the clause.[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-FOOTNOTELaka199696%E2%80%93972.2.4._A_few_full_paradigms_and_how_to_use_them-16) In earlier stages, the relation between _hik_ and _zuk_ was like [that of _you_ and _thou_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T-V_distinction "T-V distinction") in early modern English. Most Basque speakers already avoid _hi_ as too disrespectful, and its use has been diminishing. In practice, the _hika_ forms are more frequent when addressing males than females. A perception developed that associates _hika_ to spontaneity, peasantness, directness, values linked to Basque rural males, while the formal forms are used by women. It has been explained as a consequence of the [rural exodus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rural_exodus "Rural exodus") of Basque peasants. Men would become workers in a factory with other men from their town. Females would become maids, waitresses, shop clerks where informal Basque would be felt improper. When institutions have tried to nuance closeness in their public communications, the male forms have been chosen.[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-Bereziartua-17)

Non-sexism supporters propose substituting those forms by the more formal ones: _zuk duzu_ "you have it".[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-Bereziartua-17) Recently, some Basque feminists have tried to revive the use of _hika_ forms among women.[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-Bereziartua-17)

### Finnish

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Finnish")]

[Finnish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finnish_language "Finnish language"), like most other [Uralic languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uralic_languages "Uralic languages"), is mostly a gender-neutral language. Pronouns lack grammatical gender, with "hän" as the sole third-person singular pronoun. However, there are examples of [androcentrism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Androcentrism "Androcentrism") in many Finnish terms with person reference, e.g. masculine expressions being used in a generic manner to refer to both sexes. They can be [compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compound_word "Compound word") (e.g. _palomies_ lit. fireman, fire fighter), [derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morphological_derivation "Morphological derivation") (e.g. _veljeillä_<_veli_ lit. brother, to fraternize), phrases such as _uskottu mies_ (trustee, executor, administrator), idioms (e.g. _olla oma herransa_, to be one's own master), and proverbs (e.g. _auta miestä mäessä, älä mäen alla_; help a man on the hill, not under the hill). [[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-18)

With the rise of gender neutrality, many compound words have been converted to gender-neutral forms. This can be done with several strategies:[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-19)

*   Replacing the word _mies_ (man) with ''henkilö'' (person), e.g. _esimies_ (lit. foreman) becomes _esihenkilö_ (foreperson).
*   Replacing the word _mies_ with _työntekijä_ (worker), e.g. _varastomies_ (lit. warehouse man) becomes _varastotyöntekijä_ (warehouse worker).
*   Adding the suffix _-ja_ to refer to a person doing the verb's action, e.g. _johtomies_ (lit. lead man) becomes _johtaja_ (leader).

### Georgian

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Georgian")]

In [Georgian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgian_language "Georgian language"), neither pronouns nor nouns have grammatical gender. The third person pronoun "ის"(is) means he, she or it.

### Pulaar

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Pulaar")]

[Pulaar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulaar_language "Pulaar language") lacks gender pronouns such as he/she. For example sentence "Himo He Jam" means (He/She is good). Pulaar either bends verbs and removes the pronoun all together, or uses gender neutral "Himo".[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-20)

### Swahili

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: Swahili")]

[Swahili](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swahili_language "Swahili language") is a [Bantu language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bantu_languages "Bantu languages") spoken in many parts of [Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Africa "Africa") such as [Kenya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenya "Kenya") and [Tanzania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanzania "Tanzania"). It is largely gender neutral in specific nouns. Words such as actor/actress (mwigaji wa hadithi) and waiter/waitress (mtumishi mezani) are gender neutral among most others in the language. The words he, him, she, her translate to a single word in Swahili, yeye.

There are gender specific words for man/woman (mwanamume/mwanamke) and mother/father (mama/baba), so it is not completely gender neutral, although a vast majority of the words do not distinguish between male or female. The language does not have a grammatical gender either.[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-21)

### Tagalog

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages&action=edit&section=12 "Edit section: Tagalog")]

[Tagalog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tagalog_language "Tagalog language"), like most [Austronesian languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austronesian_languages "Austronesian languages"), is a [genderless language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genderless_language "Genderless language"). The third-person pronoun _siya_ is used for both "he" and "she", as well as "it" in the context of being a neuter gender.[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-22) Native nouns also feature this characteristic, normally with the addition of _lalaki_ ("male") or _babae_ ("female") to the noun to signify gender in terms such as _anak na lalaki_ ("son") or _babaeng kambing_ ("she-goat").[[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-23)

However, because Tagalog has had over three centuries of [Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_language_in_the_Philippines "Spanish language in the Philippines") influence, gender is usually differentiated in certain Spanish loanwords by way of the [suffixes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suffix "Suffix")_-a_ (feminine)and _-o_ (masculine).[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-Atleas-24) These words mostly refer to ethnicities, occupations, and family. Some examples are: _Pilipina_/_Pilipino_ (Filipina/o) and their derivative nicknames _Pinay_/_Pinoy_, _tindera_/_tindero_ (vendor), _inhinyera_/_inhinyero_ (engineer), _tita_/_tito_ (aunt/uncle), _manang_/_manong_ (elder sister/brother), and _lola_/_lolo_ (grandmother/grandfather). A few gender-differentiating pairs originate from Chinese, mostly relating to kinship terminology such as _ate_ (big sister) and _kuya_ (big brother).

The [gender neutral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality "Gender neutrality") term 'Filipinx' has gained popularity especially among Filipino-Americans as a [demonym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demonym "Demonym") or an [adjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjective "Adjective"). Since then it has been controversial, with Filipinos living in the Philippines arguing that the term 'Filipino' is already genderless. While the word is borrowed from Spanish where suffixes indicate gender, the term borrowed into Tagalog is already used in a gender-neutral manner. The coined term is said to be unnecessary and that it imposes [eurocentric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurocentrism "Eurocentrism") standards in the language.[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-25)

### Turkish

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section: Turkish")]

[Turkish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_language "Turkish language") is a gender-neutral language, like most other [Turkic languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkic_languages "Turkic languages"). Nouns have a generic form and this generic form is used for both males and females. For example, _doktor_ (doctor), _eczacı_ (pharmacist), _mühendis_ (engineer) etc. Very few words for person reference contain a clue to the gender of the referred person, such as _anne/baba_ "mother/father", _kız/oğlan_ "girl/boy", _hanım/bey_ "lady/sir". The third person singular pronoun "o" refers to "he", "she" and "it".[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-braun1-1)

At the same time research has shown a significant presence of semantically implied gender ([covert gender](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covert_gender "Covert gender")) in Turkish. In addition to the absence of semantic [gender neutrality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutral "Gender neutral") it was also noted that the usage of gender markings in Turkish is asymmetrical. In translations of sentences from English texts where the gender is evident (e.g., usage of he/she or male vs. female context) it was noticed that feminine gender was marked in 50% of cases, while masculine was marked only in 5% of cases. While translations are not typically representative of linguistic data, similar asymmetry was also observed in Turkish literary and newspaper texts.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-braun1-1)[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-26)

### Varieties of Chinese

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages&action=edit&section=14 "Edit section: Varieties of Chinese")]

See also: [Chinese pronouns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_pronouns "Chinese pronouns")

Sinitic languages (or topolects) are largely gender-neutral. Chinese has no inflections for gender, tense, or case, so comprehension is almost wholly dependent on [word order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syntax "Syntax"). There are also very few, if any, derivational inflections; instead, the language relies heavily on compounding to create new words. A Chinese word is thus inherently gender-neutral, and any given word can be preceded by an morpheme indicating masculinity or femininity. For example, the word for "doctor" is _yīshēng_ ([Traditional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_Chinese_characters "Traditional Chinese characters"): 醫生, [Simplified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplified_Chinese_characters "Simplified Chinese characters"): 医生). To specify the gender of the doctor, the speaker can add the morpheme for "male" or "female" to the front of it. Thus, to specify a male doctor, one would prefix _nán_ 男 (male), as in _nányīshēng_ (男醫生/男医生); to specify a female doctor, one would prefix _nǚ_ 女 (female), as in _nǚyīshēng_ (女醫生/女医生). Under normal circumstances, both male and female doctors would simply be referred to as _yīshēng_ (醫生/医生).

#### Mandarin

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages&action=edit&section=15 "Edit section: Mandarin")]

Spoken Mandarin Chinese also has only one third-person singular pronoun, _tā_ for all referents. _Tā_ can mean "he" (also "[He](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/He_(pronoun)#Deities "He (pronoun)")" for deities, written differently), "she", or "it". However, the different meanings of _tā_ are written with different [characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_character "Chinese character"): "他", containing the human [radical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radical_(Chinese_character) "Radical (Chinese character)") "亻", from "人", meaning person, for _he_ or a person of undetermined gender; "她", containing the feminine radical "女", for "she"; and "它" for "it"; "祂" containing the spirit radical "礻", from "示", for deities; "牠" containing the cow radical "牜", from "牛", for animals.[[27]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-27)[[28]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-:0-28)

The character for "she", containing the "woman" radical (glyphic element of a character's composition), was invented in the early twentieth century due to western influence; prior to this, the character indicating "he" today was used for both genders: it contains the "person" radical, which, as noted above, is not gender-specific.[[28]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-:0-28)

#### Cantonese

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages&action=edit&section=16 "Edit section: Cantonese")]

In [Cantonese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantonese_pronouns "Cantonese pronouns"), the third-person singular pronoun is _keui 5_, written as [佢](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E4%BD%A2 "wikt:佢"); it may refer to people of any gender because Cantonese does not have gendered third-person pronouns as in English. Replacing the "亻" radical with "女" (in pronoun [佢](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E4%BD%A2 "wikt:佢")) forms the character [姖](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%A7%96 "wikt:姖"), has a separate meaning in [written Cantonese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Written_Cantonese "Written Cantonese").[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_neutrality_in_genderless_languages#cite_note-29)
