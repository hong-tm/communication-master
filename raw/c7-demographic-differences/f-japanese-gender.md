From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Gender expression in the Japanese language

The [Japanese language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_language "Japanese language") has some words and some grammatical constructions associated with men or boys, while others are associated with women or girls. Such differences are sometimes called "gendered language".[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-OSS-1):10 In Japanese, speech patterns associated with women are referred to as _onna kotoba_ (女言葉; "women's words") or _joseigo_ (女性語; "women's language"), and those associated with men are referred to as _danseigo_ (男性語; "men's language").[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-2)

In general, the words and speech patterns associated with men are perceived as rough, vulgar, or abrupt, while those associated with women are considered more polite, more deferential, or "softer". Some linguists consider the description of "rough–soft continuum" more accurate than the description of "male–female continuum". For example, [Eleanor Harz Jorden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleanor_Harz_Jorden "Eleanor Harz Jorden") in _[Japanese: The Spoken Language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese:\_The\_Spoken\_Language "Japanese: The Spoken Language")_ refers to the styles as "blunt/gentle", rather than male/female.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-3)

There are no gender differences in written Japanese (except in quoted speech), and almost no differences in polite speech (_[teineigo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teineigo "Teineigo")_).[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-4)

## Conventional women's speech

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Gender_differences_in_Japanese&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Conventional women's speech")]

The word _onnarashii_ (女らしい), which is usually translated as "ladylike" or "feminine," refers to the behaviour expected of a typical Japanese woman in a customary scene. As well as behaving in particular ways, being _onnarashii_ means conforming to particular styles of speech. Some of the features of women's speech include speaking in a higher [register](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Register_(sociolinguistics) "Register (sociolinguistics)"), using more polite forms and using polite speech or honorifics in more situations, and referring to themselves and those whom they address more formally.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-Siegal-5)[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-6)

Some linguistic features commonly associated with women include: omission of the [copula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copula_(linguistics) "Copula (linguistics)")_da_, the use of first person [pronouns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_pronouns "Japanese pronouns") such as _watashi_ or _atashi_ among others, use of feminine sentence-final particles such as _wa_, _na no_, _kashira_, and _mashō_, and the more frequent use of the [honorific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honorific_speech_in_Japanese "Honorific speech in Japanese") prefixes _o-_ and _go-_.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-Siegal-5)

Actual language used by Japanese-speaking women differs from these ideals. Such _onnarashii_ speech is a [social norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_norm "Social norm") that institutions such as [education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Educational_institution "Educational institution") and [media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Media_(communication) "Media (communication)") encourage women to adopt. Similarly, these forms may be prescribed for women learners by Japanese textbooks and other materials. There are, however, various deviations from these norms in conversation.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-Siegal-5)

Although Japanese women may not follow the gender norm in speech, some linguistic studies indicate that Japanese women tend to use more honorific language than men do, which reinforces the idea of _onnarashii_ and conventional [gender roles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_role "Gender role").[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-7)

### Sentence-final particles

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Gender_differences_in_Japanese&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Sentence-final particles")]

One of the sentence-final particles often found in women's speech in Japan is _wa_. It is generally used in a conversation in which the speaker and addressee are close to each other. On the other hand, it is inappropriate to use _wa_ in an official relationship such as business relationships or formal interviews. The _wa_ found in women's speech has a rising intonation, while the _wa_ with falling intonation can be used by male speakers in modern standard colloquial Japanese. However, both serve the same function of conveying a sense of insistence on the part of the speaker.[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-8) One possible explanation of the femininity associated with _wa_ is the high and gentle intonation which appropriates the insistence to the better judgement of the addressee.[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-9) However, other researchers argue that the rising and high intonation are used to show an interest in the conversation and therefore help to continue it because falling intonation is often associated with ending a conversation.[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-10)

## Conventional men's speech

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Gender_differences_in_Japanese&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Conventional men's speech")]

Just as there are modes of speaking and behaviour that have sometimes been considered intrinsically feminine, there are also those that are considered _otokorashii_ (男らしい; "manly" or "masculine"). Based on men's reports of their own speech, as well as [prescriptive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prescriptive "Prescriptive") advice for language use, men's language is thought of as using fewer polite forms, distinct pronouns and sentence-final particles, and some [reduced vowels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vowel_reduction "Vowel reduction").[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-11)

Some words associated with men's speech include: the informal _da_ in place of the copula _desu_, first person pronouns such as _ore_ and _boku_, and sentence-final particles such as _yo_, _ze_, _zo_, and _kana_.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-Siegal-5) Masculine speech also features less frequent use of honorific prefixes and fewer _[aizuchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aizuchi "Aizuchi")_ response tokens.[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-Sreetharan-12)

Research on Japanese men's speech shows greater use of "neutral" forms, forms not strongly associated with masculine or feminine speech, than is seen in Japanese women's speech.[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-Sreetharan-12)

Some studies of conversation between Japanese men and women show neither gender taking a more dominant position in interaction. Men, however, tend to show a "self-oriented conversation style", telling stories and expressing their expertise on topics being discussed more than is typical of women in these studies.[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-13)

### Sentence-final particles

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Gender_differences_in_Japanese&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Sentence-final particles")]

[Sentence-final particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentence-final_particle "Sentence-final particle") that are generally considered within the realm of male speech are _zo_, _ze_ and _sa_. They are used to impart information that belongs to the speaker's sphere to an addressee. The perceived masculinity of these particles comes from the stronger level of assertive force that they have in comparison to particles like _wa_ or _no_.[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-McGloin-14):36–37 _Zo_ is the most imposing particle, stating a proposition with an authoritative tone to an addressee of lower status. It can mostly be observed among intimate male friends and within the [senior-junior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senpai_and_k%C5%8Dhai "Senpai and kōhai") social realm.[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-McGloin-14):27 _Ze_ is viewed as a milder version of _zo_ and employed by the speaker if they have a buddy-like relationship with the addressee.[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-McGloin-14)_Sa_ has a very similar status to _ze_ but the pragmatic/semantic force of it is not as clear as the other two. Attributes contributed to _sa_ are "vigorous and ego-assertive"[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-15) while others argue that _sa_ is used to state something "obvious" and "matter-of course".[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-16) Generally _sa_ implies a sense of resignation or acceptance of a given situation on the part of the speaker.[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-McGloin-14)

Work by Misao Okada shows that so-called male speech is not just used in Japanese for male identity; imperatives can be utilised in order to make the hearer perform a fast movement immediately, for instance, in a sport session.[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-Okada-17):174 Moreover, imperatives and particles with a traditional male connotation (such as _zo_) can be used to stress criticism.[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-Okada-17):177 In these contexts imperatives and other directives are part of the vocal, kinetic and material environment they occur in, making male speech dependent on a specific situation without being linked to gender.[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-Okada-17):183 These examples could demonstrate that the label "men's speech" (_danseigo_) is much more diverse than originally considered.[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-Okada-17):181[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-OSS-1):14

## In modern society

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Gender_differences_in_Japanese&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: In modern society")]

Since the late twentieth century observers have noted that individual Japanese men and women do not necessarily speak in the ways attributed to their gender. Scholars have described considerable variation within each gender; some individuals use these characteristics of gendered speech, while others do not.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-Siegal-5) Upper-class women who did not conform to conventional expectations of gendered speech were sometimes criticized for failing to maintain so-called "traditional Japanese culture".[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-Siegal-5)

### In the LGBT community

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Gender_differences_in_Japanese&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: In the LGBT community")]

Another recent phenomenon influencing gender norms in speech is the popularity of _okama_ (おかま) entertainers, typically men who enact very feminine speech, dress, and other gender markers. The word _okama_ originally referred to feminine male [homosexuals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homosexuality "Homosexuality"), but its usage has expanded to refer to masculine gay men, male [cross-dressers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transvestism "Transvestism"), and [trans women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans_woman "Trans woman"), among other uses.[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-:1-18)[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-:2-19) Entertainers who identify as _okama_ sometimes use a form of speech called _onē kotoba_ (オネエ言葉), literally "older sister speech" but with the word _[onē](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E3%82%AA%E3%83%8D%E3%82%A8 "wiktionary:オネエ")_ ("older sister") used to denote an effeminate man, a speaking style that combines the formal aspects of women's speech described above with blunt or crude words and topics.[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-shifting-20) For example:

*   あたし 今 カレー 食ったら 下痢 だ わ。

_Atashi ima karē kuttara geri da wa._"If I ate curry now, I'd get diarrhea."
The pronoun _atashi_ and the sentence-final _da wa_ is typical of women's speech, while the verb [_kuttara_](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E9%A3%9F%E3%81%86 "wikt:食う") is typical of men's speech and the topic itself is very blunt.[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-shifting-20)

Hideko Abe suggests that _onē kotoba_ originated during the [Shōwa era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sh%C5%8Dwa_era "Shōwa era") among [sex workers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sex_worker "Sex worker") known as _danshō_ (男娼), literally "male prostitutes", who adopted feminine speech, wore women's clothing, and often referred to themselves as women.[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-:0-21) Celebrities and _[tarento](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television\_personalities\_in\_Japan "Television personalities in Japan")_ who use _onē kotoba_ include [Akihiro Miwa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akihiro_Miwa "Akihiro Miwa"), Shōgo Kariyazaki, [IKKO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ikko_(make-up_artist) "Ikko (make-up artist)"), [Kaba-chan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaba-chan "Kaba-chan"), and the twin brothers Osugi and [Peeco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peeco "Peeco").[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-:0-21) In one instance, two lesbian users of _onē kotoba_ were interviewed by Claire Maree, who reported that they were characterized as _onna-onē_ (女オネエ), or "female [queens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_(slang) "Queen (slang)")" by their peers. Maree argues that they were attempting to avoid both [heteronormative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heteronormativity "Heteronormativity") femininity and stereotypes of [lesbians as masculine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butch_(lesbian_slang) "Butch (lesbian slang)").[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-22)

The word _onabe_ (おなべ), although it originally referred to masculine lesbians and female cross-dressers, has evolved to include [trans men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans_man "Trans man").[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-:1-18)[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-:2-19)[[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-23)_Onabe_[hosts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Host_and_hostess_clubs "Host and hostess clubs") interviewed in the 1995 documentary [Shinjuku Boys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shinjuku_Boys "Shinjuku Boys") use a variety of gendered markers in their speech that are traditionally considered feminine or masculine. One speaker exclusively uses the [reflexive pronoun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflexive_pronoun "Reflexive pronoun")_jibun_ (自分) as a gender-neutral first-person pronoun but also utilizes more feminine ending particles like _na no_, while another uses the masculine first-person pronoun _ore_ but does not identify as a man or a woman.[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-24) Abe further notes that two _onabe_ included in a round table discussion in the bisexual and lesbian magazine _Anise_ used _jibun_ as a first-person pronoun, while trans men preferred _boku_.[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_differences_in_Japanese#cite_note-:0-21)
