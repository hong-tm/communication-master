From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pidgin language of Malaysia

_**Bahasa Rojak**_ ([Malay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malay_language "Malay language") for "mixed [language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bh%C4%81%E1%B9%A3%C4%81 "Bhāṣā")") is a form of communication in [Malaysia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysia "Malaysia") formed by [code-switching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code-switching "Code-switching") among two or more of [its many languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_Malaysia "Languages of Malaysia") as some kind of [pidgin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pidgin "Pidgin")[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation\_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_] (trade language); _[rojak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rojak "Rojak")_ refers to a local salad which also invokes the meaning of "mixture".[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahasa_Rojak#cite_note-1)

## History

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bahasa_Rojak&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: History")]

[![Image 14](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/54/Bahaya.JPG/250px-Bahaya.JPG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Bahaya.JPG)

Warning signs at Malaysian [electrical substations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_substation "Electrical substation") still use five of the country's most common languages (top to bottom under the high voltage symbol: [Malay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malay_language "Malay language"), [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language "English language"), [Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_language "Chinese language") ([traditional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_Chinese_character "Traditional Chinese character")), [Tamil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamil_language "Tamil language"), [Punjabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punjabi_language "Punjabi language")).

Rojak language of [Malaysia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysia "Malaysia") can be traced back to 1402, in the early [Malacca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malacca "Malacca") of [Parameswara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parameswara_(sultan) "Parameswara (sultan)"), an international port where more than 80 languages from a variety of cultures were spoken. Worldwide traders, settlers, and original dwellers speaking multiple languages in a conversation was common.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation\_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

According to the _[Encyclopedia of Malaysia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclopedia\_of\_Malaysia "Encyclopedia of Malaysia")_ (_Languages and Literature_), it is a [contact language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contact_language "Contact language"), specifically a [pidgin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pidgin "Pidgin"), known in modern [Malaysia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysia "Malaysia") as rojak language.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation\_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_] The uniqueness of rojak language is in its code-switching style. A person who speaks rojak language may begin with [standard Malay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysian_language "Malaysian language"), continue with [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language "English language"), then mix one or two words in [Cantonese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yue_Chinese "Yue Chinese") garnished with [Tamil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamil_language "Tamil language"), and finish with [Mandarin Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandarin_Chinese "Mandarin Chinese") or some fashionable [Japanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_language "Japanese language") words. During Parameswara's time, when two groups of traders without a shared language met, they would try many possible languages in order to best understand each other, and the result would be a pidgin or _rojak_.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation\_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

In the early 16th century, [Portuguese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_Empire "Portuguese Empire") visitor [Tome Pires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tome_Pires "Tome Pires") found in Malacca

> "Moors from Cairo, Mecca, Aden, Abyssinians, men of Kilwa, Malindi, Ormuz, Parsis, Rumi [Turks living abroad], Turks, Turkomans, Christian Armenians, Gujaratis, men of Chaul, Dabhol, Goa, of the kingdom of Deccan, Malabars and Klings, merchants from Orissa, Ceylon, Bengal, Arakan, Pegu, Siamese, men of Kedah, Malays, men of Penang, Patani, Cambodia, Champa, Cochin China, Chinese, men from Liu Kiu [Formosa] and Brunei, Luzonese, men of Tamjompura, Laue, Bangka, Lingga (and in this area 1000 more Islands are known), from the Moluccas, Banda, Bima, Timor, Madura, Java, Sunda, Palembang, Jambi, Tongkal, Indragiri, Kappatta, Menangkabau, Siak, Arcat, Aru, Bata, from the country of the Tomjano, Pase, Pedir, from the Maldives."

These peoples came to [Malacca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malacca "Malacca") with ships, and by 1511, [Malacca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malacca "Malacca") had a population of 50,000 people, including a resident trade community that spoke 84 languages.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation\_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

An example of a multilingual signboard in [Malaysia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysia "Malaysia")

The British brought in large numbers of immigrants from [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China "China") and [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India "India") from the late 18th to mid 20th century. The presence of local Malays, [Orang Asli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orang_Asli "Orang Asli"), [Peranakans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peranakan "Peranakan"), [Portuguese settlers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kristang_people "Kristang people"), [Siamese Thais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysian_Siamese "Malaysian Siamese"), newly arrived [Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_people "Chinese people") and [Indians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_people "Indian people"), Sarawakians and Sabahans, as well as the others resulted in the wide use of mixed language.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation\_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

The [P. Ramlee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P._Ramlee "P. Ramlee") song _Dengar Ini Cerita_ (1953) notably uses Malay-English code-switching as a plot device between its subjects indicating their different social backgrounds.[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahasa_Rojak#cite_note-2)

## Examples

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bahasa_Rojak&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Examples")]

*   _Kau memang **terror** la!_ - You're really terrific!
*   _Tempat makan ni **best** sangat!_ - This food court is really cool!
*   _Nak makan sini ke nak **tapau?**_ - Do you want to dine here or take away?
*   _Jangan susah hati **maa, lu** punya bos mesti boleh **kaw tim** punya **maa!**_ - Don't worry, your boss can surely compromise!
*   _**Apasal lu** buat ini kerja **cincai?**_ - Why do you do this task sloppily?

Colloquial and contemporary usage of Malay includes modern Malaysian vocabulary, which may not be familiar to the older generation, such as:

*   _Awek_ (means girl, in place of _perempuan_).
*   _Balak_ (means guy, in place of _jantan_).
*   _Cun_ (means pretty, in place of _cantik / jelita_).

New plural pronouns have also been formed out of the original pronouns popularly nowadays and the word _orang_ (person), such as:

*   _Korang_ (_kau_ + _orang_, the [exclusive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clusivity "Clusivity") "us", in place of _kalian / kamu semua_ (or _hangpa / ampa_ in Kedah)).
*   _Kitorang_ (_kita_ + _orang_, the [exclusive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clusivity "Clusivity") "we", in place of _kami_).
*   _Diorang_ (_dia_ + _orang_, the [exclusive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clusivity "Clusivity") "they", in place of _mereka_ (or _depa_ in Kedah)).

In addition, Arabic terms that are originally used in Standard Malay nowadays have been popularly changed where some of the words or pronunciations in the involved terms have been added by the local conservative Muslims by disputing the terms suggested by the [Dewan Bahasa dan Pustaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dewan_Bahasa_dan_Pustaka "Dewan Bahasa dan Pustaka") (DBP), claiming that the involved terms with implementation of the additional words or pronunciations is the real correct terms as same as stated in the Qur'an, where it is predominantly used by the local Muslim netizens in the social media nowadays. The several involved terms in comparison to Standard Malay that is popularly used, such as:

*   _Ramadhan_ (means the holy fasting month, in place of _Ramadan_).
*   _Aamiin_ (means asking Him to verify the prayer (_Du'a_); real term is Ameen, in place of _Amin_).
*   _Fardhu_ (means obligatory (_in Islam_), in place of _Fardu_).
*   _Redha_ (means accepting, in place of _Reda_).
*   _Mudharat_ (means harm, in place of _Mudarat_).
*   _Dhaif_ (means poverty, in place of _Daif_).
*   _Zohor_ (means mid-day or noon time, in place of _Zuhur_).
*   _Hadith_ (means Prophet (Mohamed) terms or speeches, in place of _Hadis_).

### _Jangan lupa diri_

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bahasa_Rojak&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Jangan lupa diri")]

"Jangan lupa diri" ("Do not forget your roots") is a rallying cry commonly heard among Malaysians interested in protecting their linguistic heritage. This statement suggests that, regardless of race, the Malaysian people have their own roots and ancestral origin to protect. In 2002, Tun Dr. Mahathir proposed that English be 'a tool' to obtain knowledge in the sciences and mathematics, as part of [education in Malaysia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education_in_Malaysia "Education in Malaysia").

## Controversy

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bahasa_Rojak&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Controversy")]

![Image 16](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fe/Unbalanced_scales.svg/60px-Unbalanced_scales.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)This ["criticism" or "controversy" section](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Criticism#Avoid_sections_and_articles_focusing_on_criticisms_or_controversies "Wikipedia:Criticism")**may compromise the article's [neutrality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Neutral_point_of_view#Article_structure "Wikipedia:Neutral point of view")**. Please help [integrate negative information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Structure "Wikipedia:Structure") into other sections or remove [undue focus on minor criticisms and controversies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:BALASP "Wikipedia:BALASP")._(June 2025)_

Code-switching between English and Malaysian and the use of novel loanwords is widespread, forming _Bahasa Rojak_. Consequently, this phenomenon has raised the displeasure of [linguistic purists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistic_purism "Linguistic purism") in Malaysia, in their effort to uphold use of the prescribed [standard language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_language "Standard language").[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation\_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

_Bahasa Rojak_ is widely used, especially by Malaysian urban youths, which has triggered concerns about continued proficiency in the Malaysian (specifically Malay) and English languages being mixed, and consequent risks to job opportunities for new graduates. The [Malaysian government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Malaysia "Government of Malaysia") is promoting the use of standard Malay (_bahasa Melayu (baku)_) since the end of 1980s, especially in the private sector, and discouraging the usage of Bahasa Rojak, similar to the [Singapore Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singapore_Government "Singapore Government")'s [Speak Good English Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speak_Good_English_Movement "Speak Good English Movement") and its discouragement of the use of the [Singlish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish "Singlish") (Singaporean-English) pidgin. For example, Malaysian TV station [TV3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TV3_(Malaysia) "TV3 (Malaysia)") in April 2006 [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahasa_Rojak#cite_note-3) changed the name of its carnival _Karnival Sure Heboh_ to _Karnival Jom Heboh_ as a result of this concern.

[Comic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comic "Comic") magazines are often criticized for using Bahasa Rojak. Words or phrases written in Bahasa Rojak are often printed in boldface to enable readers to identify them. By the end of 2003, _[Gempak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gempak "Gempak")_ magazine began using a more formal language style and minimizing use of Bahasa Rojak, including the usage of bold lettering for words deemed colloquial.

During the Standard Malay Language Framework Congress held in November 2017,[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahasa_Rojak#cite_note-4) former Malaysian Deputy Prime Minister [Ahmad Zahid Hamidi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmad_Zahid_Hamidi "Ahmad Zahid Hamidi") expressed his disappointment at the poor usage of the national language. Despite Malaysia having achieved 60 years of independence, there are still many Malaysians (especially Malays) who could not speak proper Malay despite being born, raised, and educated in Malaysia.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahasa_Rojak#cite_note-5)[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahasa_Rojak#cite_note-6)
