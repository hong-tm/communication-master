**Singlish** (a [portmanteau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portmanteau "Portmanteau") of _Singapore_ and _English_), formally known as **Colloquial Singaporean English** (CSE) or **Singapore Colloquial English** (SCE), is an [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language "English language")-based [contact language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contact_language "Contact language") originating in [Singapore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singapore "Singapore"), and an informal register of [Singapore English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singapore_English "Singapore English") used between Singaporeans in colloquial contexts.

It linguistically resembles a [creole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creole_language "Creole language"), but its status as a creole is academically disputed. Singlish arose out of contact between speakers of different [languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_Singapore "Languages of Singapore"), mainly comprising different [English varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_varieties "English varieties"), different [Malay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malay_language "Malay language") varieties such as [Baba Malay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baba_Malay "Baba Malay") and [Bazaar Malay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bazaar_Malay "Bazaar Malay"), and different [Chinese varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varieties_of_Chinese "Varieties of Chinese") such as [Cantonese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yue_Chinese "Yue Chinese"), [Hokkien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singaporean_Hokkien "Singaporean Hokkien"), [Mandarin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singaporean_Mandarin "Singaporean Mandarin"), and [Teochew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teochew_Min "Teochew Min"). Singlish and [Standard Singapore English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singapore_English#Standard_Singapore_English "Singapore English") were modelled by academics as part of a [creole continuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creole_continuum "Creole continuum") from 1975 to 1989, but were treated distinctly from 1990 as [diglossia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diglossia "Diglossia"), with speakers employing [code-switching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code-switching "Code-switching"). As opposed to the standard variety, Singlish is mostly used in informal situations. Driven by a shared history and close geography, [Manglish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manglish "Manglish") of Malaysia closely mirrors Singlish, though there are subtle differences in vocabulary between the two.

The term _Singlish_ first appeared in newspapers and academic discourse in 1975. Singlish was initially perceived as a threat by the government; Singlish advertisements were banned from 1993, multiple political leaders spoke out against it such as by [Lee Kuan Yew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lee_Kuan_Yew "Lee Kuan Yew") in 1999, and the [Speak Good English Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speak_Good_English_Movement "Speak Good English Movement") (SGEM) was launched to discourage Singlish in 2000. From the 2010s, Singlish became more accepted; SGEM adjusted their goal to differentiate English and Singlish, Singlish advertisements reappeared, and government organisations such as the [Singapore Tourism Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singapore_Tourism_Board "Singapore Tourism Board") used Singlish. According to Mie Hiramoto, an associate professor at the [National University of Singapore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_University_of_Singapore "National University of Singapore") (NUS), this shift in stance was driven by a surge in [patriotism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriotism "Patriotism") sparked by the [50th National Day celebrations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SG50 "SG50") and the [death of Lee Kuan Yew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_of_Lee_Kuan_Yew "Death of Lee Kuan Yew") in 2015. Ng E-Ching, an English lecturer at NUS, said that this trend coincided with a growing sense of pride in Singaporean identity. Since the 2010s, it has garnered greater international attention and is frequently used by foreign artists and celebrities during local concerts or visits to embrace the local culture.

The pronunciation of Singlish is influenced by the race of the speaker and formality context. It is [syllable-timed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syllable-timed "Syllable-timed") and contains [tonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tone_(linguistics) "Tone (linguistics)") words. The [grammar of Singlish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish_grammar "Singlish grammar") includes [topic-prominence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topic-prominent_language "Topic-prominent language"), [omission of the word _be_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_copula "Zero copula"), [reduplication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reduplication "Reduplication") of words, and [discourse particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discourse_particles "Discourse particles"), which is a distinguishing feature of Singlish, such as _ah, lah_, and _what_. The vocabulary of Singlish is based on [British English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_English "British English"), with most [loanwords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loanwords "Loanwords") from Malay and Hokkien, and an increasing influence from Mandarin. The _[Oxford English Dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford\_English\_Dictionary "Oxford English Dictionary")_ has added Singlish words, such as _lah_ in its debut in 2000, _[kiasu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiasu "Kiasu")_ in 2007, and _kaypoh_ in 2026.

### Nomenclature

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Singlish&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Nomenclature")]

The term _Singaporean English_ is used extremely rarely, compared to _Singlish_ and _Singapore English_, as it has pejorative connotations showing something "typical" of a Singaporean, according to Tan Ying-Ying.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-2023-1) In literature, distinctions are made between higher and lower registers of the language, but the names used to refer to them varies by scholar, which is summarised in a table below.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-2023-1)

| Higher register | Lower register |
| --- | --- |
| Standard Singapore English (SSE) | Colloquial Singapore English (CSE) or Singapore Colloquial English (SCE) |
| Singapore English | Singlish |
| International Singapore English | Local Singapore English |

### Contrasting against standard English

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Singlish&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Contrasting against standard English")]

#### Creole continuum

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Singlish&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Creole continuum")]

[![Image 7](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c1/Illustration_of_the_creole_continuum_in_Singapore.svg/250px-Illustration_of_the_creole_continuum_in_Singapore.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Illustration_of_the_creole_continuum_in_Singapore.svg)

Platt's illustration of the [creole continuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-creole_continuum "Post-creole continuum") in Singapore

From 1975 to 1989, following John Platt's studies, scholars made no distinction between _Singapore English_ and _Singlish_, explaining differences with a [creole continuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-creole_continuum "Post-creole continuum"),[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-2023-1) focusing on class and education differences.[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Pillai-2023-2)

In the model, Singlish and [English in Singapore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singapore_English "Singapore English") exist along a [creole continuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-creole_continuum "Post-creole continuum"), ranging from standard English with local pronunciation on one end, to the most colloquial [registers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Register_(sociolinguistics) "Register (sociolinguistics)") of Singlish on the other.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Platt-1975-3) The acrolect is very similar to standard English as spoken in other English-speaking countries, with some [differences in pronunciation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singapore_English#Overview_of_Singaporean_accents "Singapore English"),[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-4) while basilectal speech is characteristic of less educated working-class Singaporeans, and a product of imperfect learning.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-2023-1) Intermediate lects are named the mesolect.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation\_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

According to Tan Ying-Ying, it is an outdated misconception, inappropriately tying class and education to the variety spoken.[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Pillai-2023-2) According to Tan Siew Imm, _Singapore English_ was negatively considered as erroneous and non-standard by scholars during this period, and views changed starting from the 1980s, recognizing it as a local variety of English.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Imm-2015-5) Leimgruber writes that the model falsely assumes that all Singaporeans can speak Singlish, and fails to account for existing attitudes contrasting two forms of English, such as in the [Speak Good English Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speak_Good_English_Movement "Speak Good English Movement").[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Leimgruber-2007-6)

#### Diglossia

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Singlish&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Diglossia")]

[![Image 8](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9a/Diglossia_model_of_Singlish_illustrated_by_Jakob_Leimgruber.png/250px-Diglossia_model_of_Singlish_illustrated_by_Jakob_Leimgruber.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Diglossia_model_of_Singlish_illustrated_by_Jakob_Leimgruber.png)

Leimgruber's illustration of [diglossia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diglossia "Diglossia") in Singapore

From 1990, following Anthea Fraser Gupta's studies, scholars made a distinction between the two varieties, explaining differences with [diglossia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diglossia "Diglossia") between two varieties,[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-2023-1) focusing on the conscious choice of style by speakers.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Imm-2015-5)

Gupta differentiates the two varieties, by observing features of [Singlish grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish_grammar "Singlish grammar"), such as the existence of [discourse particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discourse_particles "Discourse particles"), [verb groups without subjects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pro-drop_language "Pro-drop language"), [conditional clauses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conditional_clause "Conditional clause") without a [subordinating conjunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subordinating_conjunction "Subordinating conjunction"), and omission of _be_, and features of standard English, such as [subject-auxiliary inversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subject%E2%80%93auxiliary_inversion "Subject–auxiliary inversion") in [interrogatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interrogative "Interrogative"), use of [inflexions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflection "Inflection") for nouns and verbs, and "complex" verb groups involving [modal auxiliaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_verb "Modal verb").[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Gupta-1994-7):9–13

Lubna Alsagoff, however, describes the diglossia model being too narrowly categorised, and says that Singlish and standard English are points of reference of a "variational space" of language, with speakers [style-shifting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Style-shifting "Style-shifting") depending on the speech situation.[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-8) Leimgruber writes that it is not compatible with the traditional understanding of diglossia put forward by [Charles A. Ferguson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_A._Ferguson "Charles A. Ferguson"), where the low variety is native for the community regardless of social status, and no code-switching to the high variety occurs as the varieties are restricted to their own domains.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Leimgruber-2007-6) Leimgruber agreed with the diglossia model, evidencing two distinct varieties with statistical differences in the usage of discourse particles and [existential clauses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existential_clause "Existential clause").[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Leimgruber-2007-6)

### Status as a creole

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Singlish&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Status as a creole")]

Following David DeCamp's research on pidgins and creoles in 1971, scholars described English in Singapore as a pidgin.[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Bao-2015-9):2 Kenneth Ong of the [Nanyang Technological University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanyang_Technological_University "Nanyang Technological University") wrote that Singlish originated from an English-based pidgin.[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-10) David Yoong, a linguist from [La Trobe University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Trobe_University "La Trobe University"), wrote that Singlish is an English-based creole in 2009.[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Yoong-2009-11) Writers from news outlets such as _[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The\_New\_York\_Times "The New York Times")_,[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-12)_[The Straits Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The\_Straits\_Times "The Straits Times")_,[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-13)[_Time Out_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_Out_(magazine) "Time Out (magazine)"),[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-14)_[South China Morning Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South\_China\_Morning\_Post "South China Morning Post")_,[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-15) and _[AsiaOne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AsiaOne "AsiaOne")_, have written that Singlish is a creole.[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-16)

In 1995, Jacques et al. described Singlish as an "extended pidgin",[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Benjamins-1995-17):344 which resembles a creole[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Benjamins-1995-17):25 and was only beginning to gain native speakers then.[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Benjamins-1995-17):4 According to John Platt, Singlish is a "creoloid", stating that it had a similar structure to creoles, being used as a lingua franca inter-ethnically and natively, but did not develop from a pidgin.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Platt-1975-3) Ansaldo says that the term "creoloid" is controversial as it is not a "developed" terminology, and is not structurally definable.[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Ansaldo-2004-18) Bao Zhiming writes that these views were influenced by research on pidgins and creoles at the time.[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Bao-2015-9):2

According to Bao Zhiming, Singlish is not a creole due to the lack of a pidgin predecessor and English having been introduced through education.[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-19) According to David Bloom, English in Singapore cannot be described with a creole continuum, because the oldest variety of English spoken was a standard form.[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Bloom-1986-20):449

Ansaldo writes that ideas from creolisation may be used to describe how different languages played a role in the formation of English in Singapore, but should not be treated to narrowly describe Singlish as being a creole.[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Ansaldo-2004-18) Gupta uses terminology from [creology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creology "Creology"), citing its usefulness outside of its classification, which she abstains from.[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-21) Ansaldo writes that the labelling of Singlish as a creole or a "[new variety of English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Englishes "World Englishes")" does not have linguistic implications, and instead has more sociohistorical and political implications.[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Ansaldo-2004-18) Alsagoff and Jakob Leimgruber said that its classification of a pidgin, creole or "New English" was not an interesting question.[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Bao-2015-9):3

### Schneider's dynamic model

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Singlish&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Schneider's dynamic model")]

[![Image 9](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8d/Stages_of_Schneider%27s_dynamic_model.png/250px-Stages_of_Schneider%27s_dynamic_model.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Stages_of_Schneider's_dynamic_model.png)

The five evolutionary stages in [Schneider's dynamic model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schneider's_dynamic_model "Schneider's dynamic model")

In [Schneider's dynamic model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schneider's_dynamic_model "Schneider's dynamic model"), by 2007, Singapore reached the fourth stage of "endonormative stabilization" out of five stages in English language evolution, due to a general acceptance of speaking, an expression of pride, and representativeness of a national identity.[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Schneider-2007-22):160 In 2010, Bokhorst-Heng et al. agreed with the classification, citing the increased confidence and sense of ownership in the youths over Singlish and English in Singapore.[[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-23) In 2015, Tan Siew Imm agreed with the classification, citing the increasing body of research of English in Singapore, and an increased acceptability of "high variety" usage in official contexts except news and scripted dialogue, despite attempts to discourage linguistic features local to Singapore.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Imm-2015-5)

Bao Zhiming writes that while Schneider's dynamic model provides a new perspective of [New Englishes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Englishes "New Englishes"), it ignores pidgins and creoles despite being well known in [contact linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contact_linguistics "Contact linguistics"), saying that New Englishes are distinct from English-lexified pidgins and creoles.[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Bao-2015-9):4

## History

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Singlish&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: History")]

### Developmental origin

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Singlish&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Developmental origin")]

Before the arrival of the British, [Baba Malay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baba_Malay "Baba Malay"), a [creole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creole_language "Creole language") from Malay and Hokkien, was spoken by wealthy [Peranakans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peranakan_Chinese "Peranakan Chinese"), and [Bazaar Malay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bazaar_Malay "Bazaar Malay"), a pidgin[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Gupta-1994-7):41 predominantly from Malay and Hokkien,[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-2017-24) was spoken interethnically.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Gupta-1994-7):34 English was also present, likely brought over from [Malacca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malacca "Malacca") according to Ansaldo.[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Ansaldo-2004-18)

By the 1830s, British people arrived and the [English language education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language_education_in_Singapore "Language education in Singapore") was established in Singapore,[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Gupta-1994-7):35 initially dominated by Baba Malay and [Kristang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kristang_language "Kristang language")-speaking students, both of which are Malay-based contact languages,[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-:1-25) and later dominated by the Chinese population by 1921,[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Gupta-1994-7):38–39 who spoke non-Mandarin varieties.[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-:1-25) After girls started to gain access to English-language education, English became increasingly used as a native language, starting from around the 1930s.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Gupta-1994-7):46 Education taught in English gradually increased, overtaking education in Chinese in 1955,[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Bloom-1986-20) and by 1984, all education in Singapore was taught in English, with the exception of foreign community schools.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Gupta-1994-7):35–36 English was prioritised by the government for economic development, and had greater prestige.[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Wong-2021-26) In 1994, Singlish was being pioneered by children, according to Gupta.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Gupta-1994-7):47

The schools were initially dominated by European students and teachers,[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Gupta-1994-7):38–39 who taught in various varieties of Eurasian English including [British English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_English "British English"),[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Bloom-1986-20):43 which likely formed the basis of Singapore English after going through [dialect levelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialect_levelling "Dialect levelling"), according to Tan Ying-Ying.[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-2017-24) Locally educated teachers started taking over, after the establishment of the schools, which account for the large influence of Malay and non-Mandarin varieties' influence on Singlish according to Rebecca Starr, an associate professor at the [National University of Singapore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_University_of_Singapore "National University of Singapore") (NUS).[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-:1-25) Over the twentieth century, the Peranakans increasingly used English at home, which mixed with Baba Malay.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Gupta-1994-7):41 A vernacular version of Malay, based on Bazaar Malay or Baba Malay, was used extensively during conversations in English school.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Gupta-1994-7):41 Through education under the [bilingual policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bilingual_policy_in_Singapore "Bilingual policy in Singapore"), most Chinese Singaporeans focused on and spoke English instead of Mandarin, which Jock Onn Wong described as a "pragmatic" decision to enable race-agnostic communication.[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Wong-2021-26)

The development of Singlish was also accelerated by [National Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_service_in_Singapore "National service in Singapore"), which created rapport and was a common language when some could not understand English.[[27]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-27) Its ethnic neutrality, lack of mutual intelligibility between Chinese varieties, and the [bilingual policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language_planning_and_policy_in_Singapore "Language planning and policy in Singapore") also accelerated its development; Schneider says that the bilingual policy contributed the most to its development.[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Schneider-2007-22):156

According to Gupta, given the history, Singlish likely arose from varieties of standard English, such as Eurasian, [Indian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_English "Indian English"), British and [American English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_English "American English"), [varieties of Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varieties_of_Chinese "Varieties of Chinese"), such as [Hokkien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singaporean_Hokkien "Singaporean Hokkien"), [Teochew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teochew_Min "Teochew Min") and [Cantonese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantonese "Cantonese"), and a variety of Malay, such as Bazaar or Baba Malay, and possibly Kristang, the Portuguese creole.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Gupta-1994-7):45 According to Tan Ying-Ying, Singlish mostly comprises English, Bazaar Malay, Hokkien, some Cantonese, some [Tamil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamil_language "Tamil language"), and increasingly [Mandarin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singaporean_Mandarin "Singaporean Mandarin").[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-2017-24) According to Wong, Singlish arose due to the [Speak Mandarin Campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speak_Mandarin_Campaign "Speak Mandarin Campaign") restricting non-Mandarin varieties and a need to express [Southern Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Chinese "Southern Chinese") cultural values not possible in English.[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Wong-2021-26) According to Lisa Lim, the Peranakans had the greatest influence on Singlish, having developed the prosodic patterns and introduced tonal words to English.[[28]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Lim-2011-28)

In published newspapers, the term _Singapore English_ was first recorded on 22 August 1907 in _Eastern Daily Mail_ and _Straits Morning Advertiser_, and _Singlish_ was first recorded on 14 July 1975 in _[New Nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New\_Nation "New Nation")_.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-2023-1) In academics, _Singlish_ was first recorded in 1975, by John T. Platt.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-2023-1)

### Initial perception as a threat

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Singlish&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: Initial perception as a threat")]

[![Image 12](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/63/%22Get_It_Right_Poster_-Singapore%22_colour-corrected.jpg/250px-%22Get_It_Right_Poster_-Singapore%22_colour-corrected.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:%22Get_It_Right_Poster_-Singapore%22_colour-corrected.jpg)

[Speak Good English Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speak_Good_English_Movement "Speak Good English Movement")'s "Get It Right" poster at a [food court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_court "Food court") on 31 October 2010

Singlish was initially regarded as a threat to Singapore;[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Yip-2022-29) the word _Singlish_ had derogatory connotations,[[30]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-The-Straits-Times-1992-30) and was used "half-jokingly" according to Platt.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Platt-1975-3) In 1974,[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-2024-31)[Dick Lee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dick_Lee "Dick Lee")'s song, "Fried Rice Paradise", which employed Singlish,[[32]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Leong-2025-32) was banned due to "improper use of English".[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-2024-31) Singlish was deemed grammatically incorrect by the [Singapore Broadcasting Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singapore_Broadcasting_Corporation "Singapore Broadcasting Corporation") (SBC).[[33]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-1993-33) In 1999, the sitcom _[Phua Chu Kang Pte Ltd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phua\_Chu\_Kang\_Pte\_Ltd "Phua Chu Kang Pte Ltd")_ and its character Phua Chu Kang (PCK) was singled out by prime minister [Goh Chok Tong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goh_Chok_Tong "Goh Chok Tong"), warning against its use of Singlish and effects on society it could have,[[34]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-34) leading the show to reduce Singlish use.[[35]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-35) According to David Wong, the chairman of the [Speak Good English Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speak_Good_English_Movement "Speak Good English Movement") (SGEM), Singlish was becoming a patriotic symbol during PCK's stint.[[36]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Goh-2001-36)

In 2000, the SGEM was launched, an annual campaign to encourage standard English and discourage Singlish,[[37]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-37)[[38]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-38) characterising it as "bad English",[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-2017-24) fearing that it would sabotage Singapore's global economic success.[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-:1-25) Festivals were conducted holding discussions and comedy, and free automated telephone lessons on English were provided.[[36]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Goh-2001-36) It had limited success, according to Tessa Wong of _[BBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC "BBC")_.[[39]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Wong-2015-39) The SGEM was met with counter-campaigns by "well-educated" people, according to Tan Ying-Ying, such as the "Save Our Singlish Campaign", the "Speak Good Singlish Movement" and the "Sticker Lady",[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-2017-24)[Sam Lo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Lo "Sam Lo"), who pasted Singlish stickers on traffic lights.[[40]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-40)

In 1991, a [scratch-and-win](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scratch_and_win "Scratch and win") advertisement by [Kodak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kodak "Kodak") using Singlish for persuasion,[[41]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-The-Straits-Times-1991-41) described as "notorious" by _The Straits Times_,[[30]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-The-Straits-Times-1992-30) received backlash from viewers for using Singlish, who felt that it represented Singaporeans negatively.[[41]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-The-Straits-Times-1991-41) There was an increasing use of Singlish by SBC, such as in radio commercials and television programmes.[[42]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-42)

From August 1993, Singlish commercials were banned on radio and television, and Singlish programmes were restricted on television, after controversy with SBC's use of Singlish,[[33]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-1993-33) with people saying that it encouraged Singaporeans to speak badly.[[43]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-43) Singlish in advertisements was largely avoided until 2009.[[44]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Hiramoto-2019-44) According to Cheng et al. in 2008, they only found [Long John Silver's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_John_Silver's "Long John Silver's") using Singlish promotionally during the duration of the ban.[[44]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Hiramoto-2019-44) Most advertisers were unfazed by the ban, as only a minority of advertisements used Singlish regardless of the ban, according to Ivan Chong, the president of an [umbrella organisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umbrella_organization "Umbrella organization") of advertising.[[33]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-1993-33) However, a small number of advertisers said that it restricted creative freedom and more flexibility should be given.[[33]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-1993-33)

Despite the ban, Singlish was also used in an awareness campaign for the [SARS outbreak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SARS_outbreak "SARS outbreak") by PCK in 2003, due to the special occasion of a health emergency.[[44]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Hiramoto-2019-44) According to Liew Kai Khiun, a professor at [Nanyang Technological University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanyang_Technological_University "Nanyang Technological University"), the campaign was effective, and remained remembered 17 years later.[[45]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-45) After a few months, it was condemned by Goh Chok Tong again for the use of Singlish.[[44]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Hiramoto-2019-44)

In 1999, [Lee Kuan Yew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lee_Kuan_Yew "Lee Kuan Yew") had described Singlish as "a handicap we must not wish upon Singaporeans", as he promoted English for global communication and understanding.[[46]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-46) In 2001, [Lee Hsien Loong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lee_Hsien_Loong "Lee Hsien Loong") said in the SGEM that the Singaporean population was insufficient to speak a globally incomprehensible language, and that if Singlish were normalised, it will no longer be interesting or notable.[[47]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-47)

Roy Tan wrote to _[The Straits Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The\_Straits\_Times "The Straits Times")_ warning against promoting Singlish, citing Singaporeans' inability to [code-switch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code-switching "Code-switching"),[[48]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-48) and Yagger wrote to _[Project Eyeball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project\_Eyeball "Project Eyeball")_ that some well-educated Singaporeans could not tell that they were using imperfect English.[[49]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Project-Eyeball-2001-49) Dsuen wrote to _Project Eyeball_ that Singlish concealed a shameful lacklustre ability to use English.[[49]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Project-Eyeball-2001-49) Simon Ng of [_Today_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TODAY_(Singapore_newspaper) "TODAY (Singapore newspaper)") wrote that children could not differentiate Singlish and standard English.[[50]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-50) Chan Khim Soo of _[The Business Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The\_Business\_Times\_(Singapore) "The Business Times (Singapore)")_ wrote that Singlish and English could not co-exist.[[51]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Chan-2001-51) Jaime Ee of _The Business Times_ wrote that the use of Singlish by the English-educated was condescending to the Chinese-educated, as it was mockery of how they spoke.[[52]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-52)[Nicholas Lee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Lee "Nicholas Lee"), a Singaporean actor, said that only [_ah beng_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ah_Beng "Ah Beng") characters should be portrayed speaking Singlish.[[53]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-53) According to _The Straits Times_, some Singaporeans argued that it gave foreigners a negative impression of Singapore as a country that could not speak English,[[54]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-The-Straits-Times-1993-54) while Chan wrote that it would make Singapore a target of comedic ridicule.[[51]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Chan-2001-51)

Despite the official stance against Singlish, in 2006, the English-speaking [middle-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle-class "Middle-class") was the largest proponent of Singlish, using it to show an identity and authenticity.[[55]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-55) Gwee Li Sui wrote to _[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The\_New\_York\_Times "The New York Times")_ that their stance made Singlish popular among the youth.[[56]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Gwee-2016-56) Lee Kheng Hock wrote to _The Straits Times_ that Singlish showed confidence in the nation, and built rapport between Singaporeans.[[57]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-57) According to _The Straits Times_, some Singaporeans saw no problem as it was a real representation of language use in Singapore.[[54]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-The-Straits-Times-1993-54) Dick Lee said that Singapore would be deprived of culture without Singlish.[[58]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-58)

Freddie Sng and Lai Yong wrote to _The Straits Times_ that it gave Singapore a differentiating factor and that it was more easily learnable by the less-educated due to being rooted in Chinese and Malay.[[59]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Sng-1998-59) Prithpal Singh of _Today_ wrote that allowed communication which was otherwise impossible in his family.[[60]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-60) Anthony Yeo wrote to _The Straits Times_ that Singlish was crucial for non-English speaking business makers.[[61]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-61)

Lynn Lee wrote that it was even more offensive to speak in a fake and unrepresentative American accent on television.[[62]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-62) Ong Sor Fern wrote to _The Straits Times_ that [world Englishes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Englishes "World Englishes") are all different and glorifying Westerners' English was indicative of an inferiority complex.[[63]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-63) Wong Kim Hoh of _The Straits Times_ wrote that SBC themselves used Singlish elements when dubbing Chinese shows into English to bring authenticity.[[64]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-64)

[Catherine Lim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catherine_Lim "Catherine Lim"), an author, said that Singlish should be differentiated from English with bad grammar and pronunciation as a result of imperfect learning.[[30]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-The-Straits-Times-1992-30) Carolyn Wong wrote to _Today_ that Singlish and English could co-exist, but teachers had to have a good command of English.[[65]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-65) Gupta wrote that the appropriate situations for Singlish and standard English should be taught, instead of presenting standard English as a replacement for Singlish which would fail.[[66]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-66) Josh Gav wrote to _Project Eyeball_ wrote that Singlish should be left to evolve by itself.[[49]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Project-Eyeball-2001-49) Daniel Lee wrote to _Project Eyeball_ that Singaporeans were becoming victims of language policies, quoting the [Speak Mandarin Campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speak_Mandarin_Campaign "Speak Mandarin Campaign") which had encouraged Mandarin over non-Mandarin varieties for economic opportunities.[[49]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Project-Eyeball-2001-49)

### Increased acceptance

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Singlish&action=edit&section=12 "Edit section: Increased acceptance")]

From the 2010s,[[67]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Goh-2024-67) it received greater global interest[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Yip-2022-29) and became more celebrated and accepted in Singapore.[[68]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-68)[[67]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Goh-2024-67)[[69]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-2026b-69) According to Mie Hiramoto, an associate professor at NUS, the change in stance was propelled by the [death of Lee Kuan Yew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_of_Lee_Kuan_Yew "Death of Lee Kuan Yew") and the SG50 celebrations, both of which happened in 2015.[[44]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Hiramoto-2019-44) According to Ng E-Ching, an English lecturer at NUS, the increased use of Singlish accompanied the increased reputation of being Singaporean.[[70]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Sin-2017-70)

[![Image 13: Governmental tweet in 2015 using cheem](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e6/Singlish_tweet_by_the_Singapore_Government_%281%29.png/250px-Singlish_tweet_by_the_Singapore_Government_%281%29.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Singlish_tweet_by_the_Singapore_Government_(1).png)

[![Image 14: Governmental tweet in 2015 using gahmen](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/12/Singlish_tweet_by_the_Singapore_Government_%282%29.png/250px-Singlish_tweet_by_the_Singapore_Government_%282%29.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Singlish_tweet_by_the_Singapore_Government_(2).png)

[![Image 15: Governmental tweet in 2016 using paktor](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/87/Singlish_tweet_by_the_Singapore_Government_%283%29.png/250px-Singlish_tweet_by_the_Singapore_Government_%283%29.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Singlish_tweet_by_the_Singapore_Government_(3).png)

Governmental [tweets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tweet_(social_media) "Tweet (social media)"), using _cheem_ meaning _profound_ in 2015 (top), _gahmen_ meaning _government_ in 2015 (middle), and _paktor_ meaning _date_ in 2016 (bottom)[[44]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Hiramoto-2019-44)

After a courtesy campaign by PCK in 2009, which featured a reduced use of Singlish, more advertisements started appearing using Singlish, such as [McDonald's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McDonald's "McDonald's").[[44]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Hiramoto-2019-44) In 2015, the government themselves became more open to Singlish usage in advertisements, such as in promoting the [Central Provident Fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Provident_Fund "Central Provident Fund"),[[44]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Hiramoto-2019-44) and the [SG50](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SG50 "SG50") National Day celebrations.[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-2024-31) Governmental [tweets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tweet_(social_media) "Tweet (social media)") in Singlish in January 2015 was met with public outcry, but by January 2016, it did not attract attention.[[44]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Hiramoto-2019-44) Organisations such as [Mediacorp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediacorp "Mediacorp"), [National Heritage Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Heritage_Board_(Singapore) "National Heritage Board (Singapore)")[[71]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Lim-2025-71) and the [Singapore Tourist Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singapore_Tourism_Board "Singapore Tourism Board") used Singlish.[[56]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Gwee-2016-56) Organisations such as the [Singapore Police Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singapore_Police_Force "Singapore Police Force") also used Singlish, but sparingly when reaching a wider audience with more relatability and ease was deemed appropriate.[[72]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-72)

By 2016, the SGEM had adjusted their stance to encourage those who only speak Singlish to also speak standard English and sought to differentiate Singlish and standard English.[[73]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Harbeck-2016-73) Goh Eck Kheng, the chairman of the SGEM, acknowledged that Singlish was part of the Singaporean identity.[[74]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Cheng-2016-74) For example, in 2022, the SGEM created a [TikTok](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TikTok "TikTok") campaign educating people on code-switching.[[71]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Lim-2025-71)[Gwee Li Sui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gwee_Li_Sui "Gwee Li Sui"), a [literary critic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literary_critic "Literary critic"), wrote that Singlish survived the SGEM as it was used across ethnic and socioeconomic groups, unlike non-Mandarin varieties, which reduced in usage due to the [Speak Mandarin Campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speak_Mandarin_Campaign "Speak Mandarin Campaign").[[56]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Gwee-2016-56)

During the [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic "COVID-19 pandemic"), Singlish educational raps, featuring figures such as PCK, went viral and caught the attention of international media.[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-2024-31) International viewers urged their countries to create similar music.[[75]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Ratcliffe-2021-75) According to Rebecca Ratcliffe of _[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The\_Guardian "The Guardian")_, the video was described as "lovable cringe" and "surprisingly informative", and attracted concerns of overplaying.[[75]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Ratcliffe-2021-75)[Julia Chatterley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julia_Chatterley "Julia Chatterley") of _BBC_ commended the music video in an interview with [Vivian Balakrishnan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vivian_Balakrishnan "Vivian Balakrishnan").[[76]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-76) Tracy Lee of _[Channel NewsAsia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Channel\_NewsAsia "Channel NewsAsia")_ wrote that it was effective, but insulting to intelligence and embarrassing.[[77]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-77)

A Singlish [Wordle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wordle "Wordle") spin-off titled "Word-leh" was created by Sha-Mayn Teh, which Yamini Chinnuswamy of _[The Straits Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The\_Straits\_Times "The Straits Times")_ described as a "sensation" in 2022.[[78]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-78) An online Singlish dictionary titled "Chimbridge", was created in 2024 by Daniel Goh.[[79]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Rashid-2025-79) According to Lionel Wee of the [National University of Singapore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_University_of_Singapore "National University of Singapore"), the creation of "Word-leh" and "Chimbridge" indicated an increased interest among younger Singaporeans of Singlish.[[79]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Rashid-2025-79)

In 2012, during a [naturalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalization "Naturalization") ceremony, Hsien Loong endorsed the use of Singlish to integrate with society,[[56]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Gwee-2016-56) and in 2015, he said, using some Singlish, that he was glad of the increased confidence in Singapore's identity.[[71]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Lim-2025-71) Paddy Rangappa wrote to _The Straits Times_ that Singlish was a way of Singaporeans displaying ownership of English, comparing it to other dialects like [Cockney English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cockney_English "Cockney English").[[80]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-80) Wee did not blame the government for its measures against Singlish given the historical context, but said that they over-prioritised development over culture.[[79]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Rashid-2025-79) Singlish better reflected the "ethnic harmony" in Singapore compared to Bazaar Malay, albeit with an under-representation of the Indian languages, according to Tan Ying-Ying.[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-2017-24) An increasing majority of Singaporeans from surveys done in 2018 and 2024 regarded Singlish as a national identity and important part of culture,[[81]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Ang-2026-81)[[69]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-2026b-69) but Indians were the least likely to agree compared to the Chinese and Malays.[[81]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Ang-2026-81) In 2024, younger Singaporeans were most comfortable in using Singlish and most disapproving of policies that curb Singlish.[[81]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Ang-2026-81)

Peter Tan, a lecturer of English language and literature from NUS, said that Singlish usage is acceptable as long as standard English was taught well in schools.[[74]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Cheng-2016-74) Luke Lu, a [sociolinguist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociolinguist "Sociolinguist"), wrote that Singlish should be used as a teaching resource to show the difference between Singlish and English.[[82]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Lu-2016-82) Tan Siew Imm, a linguist from the [University of Canberra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Canberra "University of Canberra"), wrote that [multi-word verbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-word_verb "Multi-word verb") local to Singapore, such as _discuss about_, _emphasise on_, and _raise up_, were well-formed and should be codified instead of being classified as learner errors, which would be more helpful for academic and educational purposes than making references to British English norms.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Imm-2015-5) Wong Jock Onn wrote that both languages embodied different cultures, whose differences should be taught, such as the tendency to exaggerate in Singlish and tendency to understate in English.[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Wong-2021-26)[Zaqy Mohamad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaqy_Mohamad "Zaqy Mohamad"), then chairman of the Government Parliamentary Committee of Communications and Information, said that flexibility should be given to creative advertisements, in response to [Tower Transit Singapore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower_Transit_Singapore "Tower Transit Singapore") using Singlish on their advertisements in their buses.[[83]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-83)

Despite the weakened stance against Singlish, in 2016, Eugene Tan, an associate professor of law at the [Singapore Management University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singapore_Management_University "Singapore Management University"), warned against glorifying Singlish due to bad economic effects;[[84]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-2016-84) according to Seah Jin Wah, an "alarming" number of working Singaporeans struggled in using English.[[85]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Seah-2023-85) He compared it against cities like [Colombo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colombo "Colombo"), [Manila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manila "Manila") and [Rangoon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rangoon "Rangoon"), which lost economic power due to a deteriorating standard of English.[[84]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-2016-84) Johann Loh, the founder of Academia English & Writing Centre, wrote that the "elite" can manipulate working-class Singaporeans by using Singlish, reinforcing class structures, describing it as "false camaraderie" and [doublespeak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doublespeak "Doublespeak").[[86]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Sholihyn-2020-86) Roy Tan wrote to _The Straits Times_ that Singlish should not be encouraged nor discouraged, but efforts should be directed to improving language proficiency used for world trade.[[87]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-87)

Seah Jin Wah wrote to _The Straits Times_ that Singlish made it hard to distinguish between bad English and Singlish,[[85]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Seah-2023-85) and Eugene Tan warned against using Singlish as a reason for poor English command.[[88]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-88) Lee Hsien Loong[[70]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Sin-2017-70) and Goh Eck Kheng said that Singlish will hinder English learning, which Peter Tan disagreed with, saying that interference may occur, but proficiency cannot be affected as language acquisition cannot be reversed,[[74]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Cheng-2016-74) and Lu says that there is no scientific evidence that Singlish hinders English learning.[[82]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Lu-2016-82) Jonathan Wong wrote to _The Straits Times_ that advocates of Singlish are usually those who can code-switch, which is unrepresentative of the population.[[89]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-89) Johann Loh wrote that Singlish was a mockery of how poorer people spoke when exaggerated for comedy.[[86]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Sholihyn-2020-86) In 2017, Ong Siew Chey wrote to _The Straits Times_ disapproving the use of Singlish, saying that Singlish was for those who did not learn proper English,[[90]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-90) which was met with backlash in the comment section, with people saying that it gave them an identity.[[44]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Hiramoto-2019-44)

## Usage in society

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Singlish&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section: Usage in society")]

### Code-switching

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Singlish&action=edit&section=14 "Edit section: Code-switching")]

According to Peter Tan and Daniel Tan, by 2016, secondary school students were aware of the situations that standard English and Singlish are appropriate for.[[82]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Lu-2016-82) In informal settings, such as during conversation with friends, or transactions in [kopitiams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kopi_tiam "Kopi tiam") and shopping malls, Singlish is used without restriction.[[39]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Wong-2015-39) In a 2024 study, 71.1% of Singaporeans said that it was appropriate to use Singlish with friends.[[81]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Ang-2026-81) However, many Singaporeans hold a strong attitude against Singlish in formal settings.[[81]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Ang-2026-81)

In [Singaporean schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education_in_Singapore "Education in Singapore"), although Singlish is officially discouraged, there is often code-switching present in the classroom.[[91]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Foley-91)[[92]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-92) This is inevitable given that Singlish is the home language of many students, and many teachers themselves are comfortable with the variety.[[91]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Foley-91) In a 2024 study, 57.6% of Singaporeans said that it was inappropriate for teachers to use Singlish in class.[[81]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Ang-2026-81)

In many [white-collar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White-collar_worker "White-collar worker") workplaces, Singlish is avoided in formal contexts, especially at job interviews, meetings with clients, presentations or meetings, where standard English is preferred,[[93]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-93) due to clarity, accessibility and being understood internationally.[[81]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Ang-2026-81) In a 2024 study, 57.6% of Singaporeans said that it was inappropriate to use Singlish in e-mails, and 60.1% of Singaporeans said it was inappropriate for use in governmental speeches.[[81]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Ang-2026-81)

### In Singaporean culture

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Singlish&action=edit&section=15 "Edit section: In Singaporean culture")]

"Fried Rice Paradise", a song using Singlish by [Dick Lee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dick_Lee "Dick Lee")[[32]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Leong-2025-32) in 1974,[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-2024-31) received a TV [adaptation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adaptation_(arts) "Adaptation (arts)")[[94]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-94) and a [musical theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_theatre "Musical theatre") adaptation.[[95]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Goh-1991-95) Its ban due to "broken English" is well-known in Singapore's history, according to Faith Leong and Dynn Othman of _[Channel NewsAsia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Channel\_NewsAsia "Channel NewsAsia")_.[[32]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Leong-2025-32) "Why U So Like Dat?", another song using Singlish by [Siva Choy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siva_Choy "Siva Choy") in 1991, sold 5,000 [cassettes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassette_tape "Cassette tape") in three weeks,[[96]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-96) and was frequently played on radios.[[97]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-97)

_[Beauty World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beauty\_World\_(musical) "Beauty World (musical)")_ (1988) written by Chiang and scored by Lee,[[98]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Chew-2005-98) described as Singapore's first major musical by Julia Goh of _[The New Paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The\_New\_Paper "The New Paper")_,[[95]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Goh-1991-95) was performed in Singapore, using some Singlish in the [supporting characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supporting_character "Supporting character")' dialogue.[[98]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Chew-2005-98) In 2005, it was performed at the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago").[[98]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Chew-2005-98)_Fried Rice Paradise_ (1991) was also written by Lee using Singlish.[[99]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-99) It almost sold out about 18,000 tickets to 20 shows, being positively received by the audience according to Goh, such as through the insertion of a "local flavour".[[95]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Goh-1991-95) In 1994, the most popular theatre shows were those that used Singlish, such as _Private Parts_ by [Michael Chiang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Chiang "Michael Chiang"), outperforming other shows like _[Les Misérables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Les\_Mis%C3%A9rables\_(musical) "Les Misérables (musical)")_ and _[Cats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cats\_(musical) "Cats (musical)")_.[[100]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-100)

### In international media

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Singlish&action=edit&section=16 "Edit section: In international media")]

Since the 2020s, foreign artistes became increasingly familiar with Singlish, incorporating local terms into their [concerts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concert "Concert") in Singapore to build affinity with Singaporean concertgoers and acknowledge its mark on Singaporean identity.[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-2024-31) Notable examples include [Taylor Swift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_Swift "Taylor Swift")'s [Eras Tour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Eras_Tour "The Eras Tour"),[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-2024-31) as well as performances by [k-pop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-pop "K-pop") girl groups [I-dle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I-dle "I-dle")[[101]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-101) and [Le Sserafim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Sserafim "Le Sserafim"),[[102]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-102) and the [cantopop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantopop "Cantopop") boy band [Mirror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirror_(group) "Mirror (group)"),[[103]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-103) among other examples.[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-2024-31)

[Echo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Echo_(Overwatch) "Echo (Overwatch)") from _[Overwatch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overwatch "Overwatch")_ was released in April 2020, voiced by a Jeannie Bolet, a British voice actor,[[104]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Begum-2020-104) whose backstory was written to be Singaporean.[[105]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Frayna-2020a-105) Echo used Singlish voice lines such as _shiok_[[105]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Frayna-2020a-105) and _alamak_.[[106]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Teo-2025-106) Marion Frayna of _Geek Culture_ praised [Blizzard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blizzard_Entertainment "Blizzard Entertainment"),[[107]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Frayna-2020b-107) the company of Overwatch,[[104]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Begum-2020-104) for representing Singapore well, through its culture and language.[[107]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Frayna-2020b-107) However, some online users disapproved of Echo's overt foreignness in pronunciation,[[106]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Teo-2025-106) with some describing it as "[westernised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westernised "Westernised")"_._[[104]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Begum-2020-104) Tarvin Gill of _[Mashable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mashable "Mashable")_ described it as "an English person trying very hard to fit in".[[108]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-108) Blizzard re-recorded her voice lines in August 2020 to sound more Singaporean, after ONE Esports, a Singaporean company, released a video correcting her voice lines.[[104]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Begum-2020-104)

### In literature

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Singlish&action=edit&section=17 "Edit section: In literature")]

In 1998, literature using Singlish was not scrutinised by the public.[[59]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Sng-1998-59) Freddie Sng and Lai Yong wrote to _[The Straits Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The\_Straits\_Times "The Straits Times")_ that literature in Singlish felt more convincing and richer in culture.[[59]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Sng-1998-59)[Sylvia Toh Paik Choo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sylvia_Toh_Paik_Choo "Sylvia Toh Paik Choo"), a Singaporean humour writer, was the first to publish books on Singlish in _Eh Goondu!_ (1982) and _Lagi Goondu!_ (1986),[[109]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-109) despite not speaking Singlish.[[110]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-110) In 2000, VJ Times published _Singlish to English: Basic Grammar Guide_, a Singlish textbook.[[111]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-111)

_The Three Little Pigs Lah_ (2013) by Casey Chen, based on "[The Three Little Pigs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Three_Little_Pigs "The Three Little Pigs")", used Singlish to illustrate its humour.[[112]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-112)_Sarong Party Girls_ (2016) by Cheryl Tan, supported by the [National Arts Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Arts_Council,_Singapore "National Arts Council, Singapore"), explored sexism, and the materialistic and traditional Singaporean society, using Singlish to give the protagonist character and personality.[[113]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-113)_Spiaking Singlish: A companion to how Singaporeans Communicate_ (2017) by [Gwee Li Sui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gwee_Li_Sui "Gwee Li Sui") was written in and about Singlish, hoping that he can normalise Singlish writing, and show its possibility of non-humorous usage.[[114]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-114)

In 2002, [Colin Goh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colin_Goh "Colin Goh") and Woo Yen Yen published the _Coxford Singlish Dictionary_,[[56]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Gwee-2016-56) described as "lighthearted" by James Harbeck of [BBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC "BBC").[[73]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Harbeck-2016-73) In 2004, Jack Lee published _A Dictionary of Singlish and Singapore English_, an online resource, after encountering the language in [National Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_service_in_Singapore "National service in Singapore"), including [etymologies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etymology "Etymology") and attested sentences.[[115]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-115) In 2024, Daniel Goh launched an online dictionary, titled _Chimbridge Dictionary of Singlish and Singapore English_, which filled the gaps in existing works by including etymologies.[[116]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-116) In the _[Oxford English Dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford\_English\_Dictionary "Oxford English Dictionary")_, words such as _lah_ and _sinseh_ were included in its debut in March 2000.[[117]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Min-Kok-2016-117) In 2007, _[kiasu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiasu "Kiasu")_ was added and was selected as the word of the day on 11 February 2015.[[117]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Min-Kok-2016-117) In 2016, it added 19 new terms including _ang moh_, _shiok_ and _sabo_, and in 2026, it added 11 more, including _BTO_, _wayang_ and _kaypoh_.[[118]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-118)

### In academics

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Singlish&action=edit&section=18 "Edit section: In academics")]

According to Tan Siew Imm, Singlish is among the most well-researched [second-language varieties of English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_as_a_second_or_foreign_language "English as a second or foreign language").[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Imm-2015-5) By 2017, Singlish was considered by [linguists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistics "Linguistics") to be an independent language with its own systematic grammar,[[70]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Sin-2017-70) being studied by at least seven universities internationally.[[119]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-119) For example, in 2017, Neil Myler of the [Boston University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston_University "Boston University") offered a course on accents, dialects and society, which introduced Singlish to 60 students, and from 2007, Leimgruber introduced Singlish in sociolinguistics classes.[[70]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Sin-2017-70) Cynthia Siew, a [psycholinguist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psycholinguistics "Psycholinguistics") from the [National University of Singapore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_University_of_Singapore "National University of Singapore"), developed "A Small World of Singlish Words", a map of words including Singlish terminology, formed using a [word association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_Association "Word Association") task.[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Pillai-2023-2)

### Variation

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Singlish&action=edit&section=20 "Edit section: Variation")]

Singlish [pronunciation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pronunciation "Pronunciation"), while built on a base of [British English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_English "British English"), is heavily influenced by Malay, Hokkien and Cantonese. There are variations within Singlish, both geographically and ethnically. Chinese, Native Malays, Indians, Eurasians, and other ethnic groups in Singapore all have distinct accents,[[120]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Lim-120)[[121]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-121) and the accentedness depends on factors such as formality of the context[[122]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-122) and language dominance of the speaker.[[123]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-123) Pronunciation of Singlish is mostly similar to [Singapore English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singapore_English "Singapore English").[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Gupta-1994-7)[_[page needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing\_sources "Wikipedia:Citing sources")_]

*   Pronunciation of ⟨th⟩: As [onset consonants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syllable#Onset "Syllable"), the [dental fricatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dental_fricative "Dental fricative")–[/θ/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") and [/ð/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English")– often merge with [/t/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") and [/d/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"), so _three_ is pronounced like _tree_, and _then_ like _den_.[[125]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Bao-Zhiming-125) As [coda consonants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syllable#Coda "Syllable") (i.e., at the end of [syllables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syllable "Syllable")), they are [fronted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Th-fronting "Th-fronting") to [/f/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") and [/v/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") respectively, so _north_ is pronounced like _norf_, and _bathe_ like _bave_.[[126]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Deterding-2010-126):14 The contrast is usually maintained in acrolectal speech, though even among educated speakers there is some variation.[[127]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-127)
*   [Aspiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspirated_consonant "Aspirated consonant"): Onset [/p/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"), [/t/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") and [/k/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") are sometimes [unaspirated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unaspirated "Unaspirated"),[[125]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Bao-Zhiming-125) especially among Malays.[[128]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-128) Aspiration is retained in loanwords from [Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinitic_languages "Sinitic languages").[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation\_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
*   [L-vocalisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L-vocalisation "L-vocalisation"): Word-final or preconsonantal /l/, as in _mai**l**_–realised as a [dark _l_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velarised_alveolar_lateral_approximant "Velarised alveolar lateral approximant") in [Received Pronunciation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Received_Pronunciation "Received Pronunciation") and [American English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_American "General American")–is often so [velarised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velarization "Velarization") in Singlish that it approaches a [high](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Close_vowel "Close vowel")[back vowel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Back_vowel "Back vowel"), e.g. _sale_[seɤ̯~seu̯]. The sound tends to be lost after the back vowels /ɔ,o,u/, and sometimes /ə/, which makes _mall_ and _more_, _wall_ and _war_, and _Saul_ and _saw_[homophones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homophone "Homophone").[[129]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-129)[[130]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-130) This is not the case for some speakers with Tamil or Malay accents who may use [clear or dark "l"s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voiced_dental,_alveolar_and_postalveolar_lateral_approximants "Voiced dental, alveolar and postalveolar lateral approximants") in these environments instead.[[131]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-131)
*   Lack of [syllabic consonants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syllabic_consonant "Syllabic consonant"): Sequences like /ən/ or /əm/ are never [syllabic consonants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syllabic_consonant "Syllabic consonant") in Singlish, hence _taken_[ˈtekən], never *[ˈtekn̩].[[132]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-132):142
*   [Glottal stop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glottal_stop "Glottal stop") insertion: A [glottal stop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glottal_stop "Glottal stop")[ʔ] may be inserted at the beginning of words starting with a vowel, as in [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_German_phonology "Standard German phonology"). As a result, final consonants do not experience [liaison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liaison_(linguistics) "Liaison (linguistics)"), i.e. run onto the next word. For example, "ran out of eggs" is realised as [ɹɛn ʔau̯t ʔɔf ʔeks] for some speakers (compare [General American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_American "General American")[ɹɛən‿aʊɾ‿əv‿ɛɡz]), with glottal stops in lieu of [null onsets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null_onset "Null onset").[[133]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Brown-133)
*   [Glottal replacement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glottalization#Glottal_replacement "Glottalization"): Though uncommon in modern speakers, a glottal stop [ʔ] may replace final stop consonants (except /p,b/), especially in fast-paced speech: _Goodwood Park_ becomes _Gu'-wu' Pa'_[ˈɡuʔ ˈwuʔ ˈpaʔ], _exist_ is realised as [ɛʔˈzist], and there may be a glottal stop at the end of words like _back_ and _out_.[[134]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-134)
*   [Final-obstruent devoicing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Final-obstruent_devoicing "Final-obstruent devoicing"): Word-finally, the distinction between voiced and voiceless sounds, i.e. [/s/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") – [/z/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"), [/t/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") – [/d/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"), etc., is sometimes not maintained. As a result, _cease_ = _seize_/sis/ and _race_ = _raise_/res/ for some speakers.[[135]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Deterding-135)
*   [Cluster reduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cluster_reduction "Cluster reduction"): Final consonant clusters may simplify, especially in fast speech.[[136]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-136) In general, [plosives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plosive "Plosive"), especially [/t/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") and [/d/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"), are lost if they come after another consonant that is non-lateral, e.g. _want_ = _one_/wan/, _tact_ = _tack_/tɛk/. Additionally, [/k/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") may be deleted after [/s/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"), e.g. _flask_/flas/.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation\_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

### Vowels

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Singlish&action=edit&section=22 "Edit section: Vowels")]

Broadly speaking, there is a one-to-many mapping of Singlish vowel [phonemes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoneme "Phoneme") to British [Received Pronunciation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Received_Pronunciation "Received Pronunciation") vowel phonemes, with a few exceptions (as discussed below, with regard to _egg_ and _peg_). The following describes a typical system.[[125]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Bao-Zhiming-125)[[137]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-137)[[138]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-138) There is generally no distinction between the non-close front [monophthongs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monophthong "Monophthong"), so _pet_ and _pat_ are pronounced the same /pɛt/.[[139]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-139)

At the acrolectal level, the merged vowel [phonemes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoneme "Phoneme") are distinguished to some extent. These speakers make a distinction between the tense vowels /i,u/ (FLEECE, GOOSE) and the lax vowels /ɪ,ʊ/ (KIT, FOOT) respectively. Some speakers introduce elements from [American English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_English "American English"), such as pre-consonantal [ɹ] (pronouncing the "r" in _bi**r**d_, _po**r**t_, etc.).[[140]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-140) This is caused by the popularity of American TV programming.[[141]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-141) In 2007, about 20 per cent of university undergraduates sometimes use this American-style pre-consonantal [ɹ] when reading a passage.[[142]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-142)

*   _Next_–_text_ split: For many speakers, some words, including _leg_ and _bed_, have the raised vowel /e/, instead of /ɛ/.[[143]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-143) This is not entirely predictable, as _egg_ has a close vowel (so it rhymes with _vague_) while _peg_ has an open vowel (and rhymes with _tag_); and similarly for most speakers _bed_ has a close vowel (so it rhymes with _made_), while _fed_ has a more open vowel (the same vowel as in _bad_). The word _next_ has the raised vowel and does not rhyme with _text_.[[135]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Deterding-135) Which vowel occurs in each word therefore appears in these cases not to be predictable. This is illustrated by the fact that _red_/red/ and _read_ (past tense) /rɛd/ are not [homophones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homophone "Homophone") in Singlish. This split only applies to the diaphoneme /ɛ/.
*   /ai/ remains /ai/ in Singlish, except when followed by /l/, in which case it is the monophthong /a/.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation\_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
*   Examples of words that have idiosyncratic pronunciations: _flour_/fla/ (expected: /flau.ə/ = _flower_);[[144]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-144) and _their_/ðja⁓dja/ (expected: /ðɛ⁓dɛ/ = _there_). _Flour_/_flower_ and _their_/_there_ are therefore not homophones in Singlish.
*   In general, Singlish vowels are tenser–there are no lax vowels (which RP has in _pit_, _put_, and so forth).[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation\_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
*   The vowels in words such as _day_/de/ and _low_/lo/ are pronounced with less glide than the comparable [diphthongs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diphthong "Diphthong") in RP, so they can be regarded as [monophthongs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monophthong "Monophthong")–i.e. vowels with no glide.[[145]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-145)[[146]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-146)
*   Where other varieties of English have an unstressed /ə/, i.e. a reduced vowel, Singlish tends to use the full vowel based on orthography. This can be seen in words such as _**a**ccept_/ɛksɛp/, _**e**xample_/ɛ(k)sampə(l),ɛ(k)zampə(l)/, _purch**a**se_/pətʃes/, _maint**e**nance_/mɛntenəns,mentenəns/, _pr**e**s**e**ntation_/prizɛnteʃən/, and so on. However, this does not mean that the reduced vowel /ə/ never occurs, as _about_ and _again_ have /ə/ in their first syllable. It seems that the letter 'a' is often pronounced /ə/, but the letter 'o' usually has a full vowel quality, especially in the _con-_ prefix (_control_, _consider_, etc.).[[147]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-147) There is a greater tendency to use a full vowel in a syllable which is closed off with a final consonant, so a full vowel is much more likely at the start of _**ab**sorb_/ɛbzɔb/ than _**a**fford_/əfɔd/.[[148]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-148)
*   In loanwords from [Hokkien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Min_Nan "Min Nan") that contain [nasal vowels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasal_vowel "Nasal vowel"), the nasalisation is often kept–one prominent example being the [mood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_mood "Grammatical mood")[particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_particle "Grammatical particle")_hor_, pronounced [hõ˨].[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation\_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

### Tone and prosody

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Singlish&action=edit&section=23 "Edit section: Tone and prosody")]

Singlish is [syllable-timed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timing_(linguistics) "Timing (linguistics)") compared to most varieties of English, which are usually stress-timed.[[149]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-149)[[150]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-150)[[151]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-151) Singlish intonation is different from intonation in British English, due to an unclear position of a [nucleus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleus_(linguistics) "Nucleus (linguistics)") within an [intonational phrase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intonational_phrase "Intonational phrase").[[152]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Deterding-2-152) Unlike American English, Singlish employs more prominent syllables and assigns the same amount of stress to syllables following a prominent syllable, such as in [enclitics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enclitics "Enclitics").[[153]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-153) Its intonation is influenced by Malay, and the extent of its influence varies by the speaker's ethnicity.[[126]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Deterding-2010-126):38

Content words are typically stressed, and function words are typically unstressed with some exceptions.[[152]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Deterding-2-152) Repeated use of a content word do not undergo de-stressing unlike standard English.[[152]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Deterding-2-152)[Demonstratives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demonstrative "Demonstrative") such as _this_ and [modals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_verb "Modal verb") such as _should_ receive stress.[[152]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Deterding-2-152) Sentence-final pronouns receive heavy stress.[[126]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Deterding-2010-126):33 Similar stress is given to each syllable in most bisyllabic words such as _language_, with exceptions such as _basic_ which has more stress on the first syllable.[[152]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Deterding-2-152) Trisyllabic words may have equal stress on all syllables, such as in _continue_, or on only some syllables, such as in the second syllable of _remember_.[[152]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Deterding-2-152) The final syllable of an utterance is often dragged out, similar to Malay,[[126]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Deterding-2010-126):38 as an indicator of prominence, and may be analysed as a level tone.[[154]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-:2-154)

Words of [Sinitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinitic_languages "Sinitic languages") origin retain their original tones in Singlish, such as _[kiasu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiasu "Kiasu")_ which maintains its original Hokkien tones, with the exception of words that are loaned via English first, such as _Taiwan_, which is said without tone.[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Wong-2021-26)

Pitch contour of a declarative sentence spoken by a [Chinese Singaporean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Singaporeans "Chinese Singaporeans"), from Chong (2012). Here, "aL" and "Ha" mark the left and right edges of an [accentual phrase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonological_hierarchy "Phonological hierarchy"), and [L*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ToBI#Tonal_events "ToBI") is a [pitch accent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitch_accent_(intonation) "Pitch accent (intonation)") falling on stressed syllables. The gradual downwards movement of pitch towards the end of the sentence is represented by the [boundary tone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_tone "Boundary tone") L%.[[155]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Chong-2012-155)

Singlish is governed by [pitch accent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitch_accent_(intonation) "Pitch accent (intonation)").[[155]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Chong-2012-155) It may be syllabically analysed using low, middle, and high tones.[[156]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Ng-2009-156) Tones do not glide gradually between tones, and instead move in sustained steps.[[154]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-:2-154)[Syllables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syllable "Syllable") with [stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress_(linguistics) "Stress (linguistics)") receive a middle tone according to Ng E-Ching,[[156]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Ng-2009-156) and an "[L*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ToBI "ToBI") pitch accent", referring to a drop in tone, according to Adam Chong.[[155]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Chong-2012-155) A low tone is assigned to the first syllable of a [prosodic word](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prosodic_word "Prosodic word") if it is unstressed.[[156]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Ng-2009-156) A high tone is assigned to the last syllable of a prosodic word;[[157]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Ng-2012-157) some words such as _watermelon_ may be split into _water_ and _melon_, receiving a high tone at the end of _water_.[[155]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Chong-2012-155) Word-final high tones are a feature ultimately from indigenous Malay, through Bazaar Malay, with reinforcement from Baba Malay and [Indian English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_English "Indian English").[[157]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Ng-2012-157) All other syllables are either given the middle tone or left unspecified.[[156]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Ng-2009-156)

Tones may also be analysed as occurring over an entire word, as opposed to a syllabic analysis.[[152]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Deterding-2-152) A rising tone is most commonly used, accompanied by a long sequence of rising tones, and occurs rarely in the final position.[[152]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Deterding-2-152) A falling tone is used to emphasise a word or indicate the completion of a thought, which may mark the end of an [intonational phrase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intonational_phrase "Intonational phrase").[[152]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Deterding-2-152) A lengthened falling tone additionally includes emotions such as disappointment.[[154]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-:2-154) A rise-fall tone is used for emphasis, usually appearing in the final position,[[152]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Deterding-2-152) and may convey strong approval, strong disapproval, or surprise similar to British English.[[154]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-:2-154) A level tone is used for [filler words](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filler_words "Filler words") and signals an incomplete thought.[[152]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Deterding-2-152) A rise-level tone, while rarer, is also used for an incomplete thought.[[154]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-:2-154) A fall-level tone is used to express a viewpoint believed to be correct with an expectation of a comment from the speaker.[[154]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-:2-154) A level-fall tone is used to put more emphasis on an emotion.[[154]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-:2-154)

[![Image 20](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ef/Singlish_prosodic_structure_by_Adam_J._Chong.png/250px-Singlish_prosodic_structure_by_Adam_J._Chong.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Singlish_prosodic_structure_by_Adam_J._Chong.png)

[Prosodic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prosody_(linguistics) "Prosody (linguistics)") structure of Singlish proposed by Chong

[Accentual phrases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accentual_phrase "Accentual phrase"), which may be a word or slightly longer, start with a low tone and end with a high tone.[[155]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Chong-2012-155) Intermediate and full intonational phrases are marked with a [boundary tone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_tone "Boundary tone"), which can be low in [declaratives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declarative_sentence "Declarative sentence") and [wh-questions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wh-questions "Wh-questions") and high in [yes-no questions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yes/no_question "Yes/no question").[[155]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Chong-2012-155) A "boosted pitch" or "early booster" often occurs at the start of a sentence, such as in sentences that start with _actually_ or _I think_,[[126]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Deterding-2010-126):35 similar to the "high key" used in British English to signal a new topic.[[152]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Deterding-2-152) David Deterding wrote that a [prosodic unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prosodic_unit "Prosodic unit") begins with a boosted pitch and ends with a falling or rise-fall tone.[[152]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Deterding-2-152) Questions may end in more commonly, a falling tone, and less commonly, a rise-fall tone.[[154]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-:2-154) Sharp falling tones are associated with strong disagreement or surprise.[[154]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-:2-154)[Imperatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperative_sentence "Imperative sentence") and exclamations more commonly use a falling or rise-fall tone.[[154]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-:2-154)

The pronunciation of Singlish is described as having a "[staccato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staccato "Staccato")" effect by multiple linguists such as Platt,[[133]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Brown-133) and others also say it has a "sing-song quality", according to Adam Chong.[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-:1-25) However, Adam Brown says that it is a musical term with no linguistic definition.[[133]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Brown-133) Brown attributed Singlish having shortened vowels, along with no [stress-timing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress-timing "Stress-timing") and [liaison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandhi "Sandhi"), to it being described as "staccato".[[133]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Brown-133)

Overall, the differences between the different ethnic communities in Singapore are most evident in the patterns of intonation. For example, Malay Singaporeans often have the main pitch excursion later in an utterance, unlike ethnically Chinese and Indian Singaporeans.[[120]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Lim-120)[_[verification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability "Wikipedia:Verifiability")_]

## Grammar

Main article: [Singlish grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish_grammar "Singlish grammar")

[![Image 21](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6e/Syntax_tree_of_%22the_cake_John_always_buy_one%22.png/250px-Syntax_tree_of_%22the_cake_John_always_buy_one%22.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Syntax_tree_of_%22the_cake_John_always_buy_one%22.png)

[Parse tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parse_tree "Parse tree") of _the cake John always buy one_, with _one_ analysed as a [complementiser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complementiser "Complementiser"), illustrated by Bao

[Singlish grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish_grammar "Singlish grammar") is the set of structural rules Singlish follows, including the structure of words, phrases, clauses, and sentences. It is influenced by the grammar of [Malay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahasa_Melayu "Bahasa Melayu") and the [Chinese languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_languages "Chinese languages").[[158]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-158)

Some features of Singlish grammar include [topic-prominence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topic-prominent_language "Topic-prominent language"), where sentences begin with a known [referent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Referent "Referent") of the conversation,[[159]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-159)[omission of the word _be_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_copula "Zero copula"),[[126]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Deterding-2010-126):51[reduplication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reduplication "Reduplication") of words,[[160]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-160) and [discourse particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discourse_particles "Discourse particles") such as _ah, lah_, and _what_.[[161]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Lim-2007-161) Discourse particles are a distinguishing feature of Singlish,[[162]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-162) which impart [pragmatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pragmatics "Pragmatics") but not [semantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantic "Semantic") information.[[163]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-163) According to Smakman and Wagenaar, _lah_ and _ah_ are used the most.[[164]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-164) The earliest particles, _ah_, _lah_, and _what_, were acquired from [Hokkien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hokkien "Hokkien") and [Bazaar Malay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bazaar_Malay "Bazaar Malay"), while a later set of particles, _lor_, _hor_, _leh_, _meh_ and _mah_, were acquired mostly from [Cantonese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantonese "Cantonese").[[161]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Lim-2007-161)

## Vocabulary

Further information: [Singlish vocabulary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish_vocabulary "Singlish vocabulary")

Much of Singlish vocabulary is derived from [British English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_English "British English"),[[165]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Brown-2001-165):12 in addition to loanwords from [Malay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malay_language "Malay language"), [Hokkien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singaporean_Hokkien "Singaporean Hokkien"), [Mandarin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singaporean_Mandarin "Singaporean Mandarin"), [Cantonese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantonese "Cantonese") and [Tamil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamil_language "Tamil language").[[166]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Lai-2025-166) Malay and Hokkien contributed the most loanwords to Singlish.[[166]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Lai-2025-166)[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-2017-24) Of the Chinese varieties, Mandarin is a newer influence on Singlish compared to Hokkien and Cantonese, which have had a longer history and greater influence._[[165]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite\_note-Brown-2001-165)_:viii The composition is described as a "fair representation" of languages spoken in Singapore, except the Indian languages, by Tan Ying-Ying.[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-2017-24) It also shares some words with [American English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_English "American English") such as _Scotch tape_, compared to British English _Sellotape.[[165]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite\_note-Brown-2001-165)_:12–13

According to a 2024 study surveying Singaporeans between 16 to 59 years old by [Preply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preply "Preply"), a language-learning company, the most popular terms in Singlish were _alamak_, an expression of alarm, _makan_, meaning to eat, and the [discourse particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish_grammar#Discourse_particles "Singlish grammar"), _lah_, _lor_, _leh_, and _meh_.[[168]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-168)

During borrowing, words of English origin sometime take on the alternative senses of their Chinese counterparts, resulting in an extension in meanings.[[169]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Deterding-2000-169) For example, _send_ can be used to mean 'accompany someone', as in "Let me send you to the airport", possibly under the influence of Hokkien 送 (_sàng_) or Cantonese 送 (_sung3_).[[169]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Deterding-2000-169)

The [lexis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lexis_(linguistics) "Lexis (linguistics)") of Singlish is very similar to [Manglish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manglish "Manglish"), although there are some differences in the frequency of usage.[[167]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Norizam-2014-167) Manglish receives more influence from Malay, such as the greater use of the suffix _-kan_ to gain clarification.[[167]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Norizam-2014-167) On the other hand, Singlish receives more influence from Chinese languages, such as the greater use of Cantonese _lor_[[167]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Norizam-2014-167) to express obviousness and resignation.[[170]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-170) This is due to the geographically close locations of Singapore and Malaysia, shared culture, and shared history.[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Yoong-2009-11)

In the 20th century, older expressions such as _angkat_ and _samseng_, which come from [Bazaar Malay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bazaar_Malay "Bazaar Malay") and [Hokkien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hokkien "Hokkien") respectively, saw less use, while words like _slay_ and _cooked_ became more prevalent, creating a generational difference between younger and older people in understanding each other's Singlish.[[171]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-2026a-171) The features from the contact languages that initially formed Singlish decreased, but the distinctiveness of Singlish increased.[[171]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-2026a-171) This was due to a shift from Bazaar Malay and Hokkien as more widely-spoken languages to English, with Hokkien diminishing due to the [Speak Mandarin Campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speak_Mandarin_Campaign "Speak Mandarin Campaign") and Bazaar Malay losing its role as a lingua franca.[[171]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-2026a-171) According to Tan Ying Ying, a [linguist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguist "Linguist") from [Nanyang Technological University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanyang_Technological_University "Nanyang Technological University"), it reflected a change adapting to a different social context.[[171]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Tan-2026a-171)

### _Kiasu_

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Singlish&action=edit&section=26 "Edit section: Kiasu")]

Main article: [Kiasu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiasu "Kiasu")

[![Image 23](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c8/Tissue_paper_pack_used_for_reserving_seats.jpg/250px-Tissue_paper_pack_used_for_reserving_seats.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Tissue_paper_pack_used_for_reserving_seats.jpg)

A pack of [tissues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tissue_paper "Tissue paper") used to _chope_ ('reserve') seats, a behaviour associated with being _[kiasu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiasu "Kiasu")_.[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Wong-2021-26)

_[Kiasu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiasu "Kiasu")_, a Singlish word[[172]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Lee-2015-172) from Hokkien 惊输 / 驚輸 (_kiaⁿ-su_), means 'afraid of losing out'.[[173]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Lim-2016-173) It first appeared formally in a parliamentary document in 1990, after having been restricted in usage to [National Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_service "National service")[[173]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Lim-2016-173) with negative connotations, but became more nuanced after.[[174]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Keating-2018-174) A [Southern Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Chinese "Southern Chinese") cultural value,[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Wong-2021-26) it refers to mindsets such as not wanting to forgo a free benefit and not wanting to fall behind peers in academics, and may be compared to the [fear of missing out](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fear_of_missing_out "Fear of missing out").[[175]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-175)

According to [Gwee Li Sui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gwee_Li_Sui "Gwee Li Sui"), being _kiasu_ is controversial; it is looked down upon but yet happily practised individually.[[174]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Keating-2018-174) Mr Kiasu, a character which debuted in 1990 created by the artist [Johnny Lau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnny_Lau "Johnny Lau"), received cultural iconicity according to Sarah Keating of the _[BBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC "BBC")_; it received a TV [adaptation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adaptation_(arts) "Adaptation (arts)") and a [McDonald's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McDonald's "McDonald's") burger based on it.[[174]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Keating-2018-174)

According to a 2015 study by the [Institute of Policy Studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_of_Policy_Studies_(Singapore) "Institute of Policy Studies (Singapore)"), _kiasu_ was the top descriptor of Singapore's society.[[176]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-176) It was added to the _[Oxford English Dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford\_English\_Dictionary "Oxford English Dictionary")_ in March 2007, and was featured as its word of the day on 11 February 2015.[[172]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlish#cite_note-Lee-2015-172)
