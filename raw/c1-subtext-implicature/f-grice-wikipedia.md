From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pragmatics of conversational communication

For the principles governing the functioning of co-operative organizations, see [Rochdale Principles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rochdale_Principles "Rochdale Principles").

In [social science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_science "Social science") generally and [linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistics "Linguistics") specifically, the **cooperative principle** describes how people achieve effective [conversational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversation "Conversation") communication in common social situations—that is, how listeners and speakers act cooperatively and mutually accept one another to be understood in a particular way.

The philosopher of language [Paul Grice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Grice "Paul Grice") introduced the concept in his [pragmatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pragmatics "Pragmatics") theory:

> Make your contribution such as is required, at the stage at which it occurs, by the accepted purpose or direction of the talk exchange in which you are engaged.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-grice-1):45

These are Grice's four **maxims of conversation** or **Gricean maxims:** quantity, quality, relation, and manner. They describe the rules followed by people in conversation.[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-Kordi%C4%87-1991-2) Applying the Gricean maxims is a way to explain the link between [utterances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utterance "Utterance") and what is understood from them.

Though phrased as a [prescriptive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistic_prescription "Linguistic prescription") command, the principle is intended as a [description](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistic_description "Linguistic description") of how people normally behave in conversation. Lesley Jeffries and Daniel McIntyre (2010) describe Grice's maxims as "encapsulating the assumptions that we prototypically hold when we engage in conversation."[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-3) The assumption that the maxims will be followed helps to interpret utterances that seem to flout them on a surface level; such flouting often signals unspoken _[implicatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversational\_implicature "Conversational implicature")_ that add to the meaning of the utterance.

## Grice's maxims

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Cooperative_principle&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Grice's maxims")]

The concept of the cooperative principle was introduced by the linguist [Paul Grice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Grice "Paul Grice") in his [pragmatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pragmatics "Pragmatics") theory. Grice researched the ways in which people derive [meaning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meaning_(philosophy) "Meaning (philosophy)") from language. In his essay _Logic and Conversation_ (1975)[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-4) and book _Studies in the Way of Words_ (1989),[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-Grice-1989-5) Grice outlined four key categories, or maxims, of conversation—quantity, quality, relation, and manner—under which there are more specific maxims and sub-maxims.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-6)[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-Penn-7)[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-Okanda_et_al-2015-8)

These describe specific [rational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rationality "Rationality") principles observed by people who follow the cooperative principle in pursuit of effective communication.[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-Effectiviology-9)[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-Kordi%C4%87-1991-2) Applying the Gricean maxims is therefore a way to explain the link between [utterances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utterance "Utterance") and what is understood from them.

According to Grice:[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-Grice-1989-5)

> Our talk exchanges do not normally consist of a succession of disconnected remarks, and would not be rational if they did. They are characteristically, to some degree at least, cooperative efforts; and each participant recognizes in them, to some extent, a common purpose or set of purposes, or at least a mutually accepted direction.
> 
> 
> This purpose or direction may be fixed from the start (e.g., by an initial proposal of a question for discussion), or it may evolve during the exchange; it may be fairly definite, or it may be so indefinite as to leave very considerable latitude to the participants (as in a casual conversation). But at each stage, _some_ possible conversational moves would be excluded as conversationally unsuitable.
> 
> 
> We might then formulate a rough general principle which participants will be expected (ceteris paribus) to observe, namely: Make your conversational contribution such as is required, at the stage at which it occurs, by the accepted purpose or direction of the talk exchange in which you are engaged. One might label this the _Cooperative Principle_. [emphasis added]
> 
> 
> On the assumption that some such general principle as this is acceptable, one may perhaps distinguish four categories under one or another of which will fall certain more specific maxims and submaxims, the following of which will, in general, yield results in accordance with the Cooperative Principle. Echoing [Kant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant "Immanuel Kant"), I call these categories Quantity, Quality, Relation, and Manner.

### Maxim of quantity (informativity)

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Cooperative_principle&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Maxim of quantity (informativity)")]

The maxim of quantity is: _be informative_.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-Penn-7)[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-Okanda_et_al-2015-8)[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-Effectiviology-9)

Submaxims:

1.   Make your contribution as informative as is required (for the current purposes of the exchange).
2.   Do not make your contribution more informative than is required.

In his book, Grice uses the following analogy for this maxim: "If you are assisting me to mend a car, I expect your contribution to be neither more nor less than is required. If, for example, at a particular stage I need four screws, I expect you to hand me four, rather than two or six."[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-Effectiviology-9)

### Maxim of quality (truth)

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Cooperative_principle&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Maxim of quality (truth)")]

The maxim of quality is: _be truthful_.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-Penn-7)[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-Okanda_et_al-2015-8)[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-Effectiviology-9)

Supermaxim:

*   _Try to make your contribution one that is true._

Submaxims:

1.   Do not say what you believe is false.
2.   Do not say that for which you lack adequate [evidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evidence "Evidence").[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-10)

In his book, Grice uses the following analogy for this maxim: "I expect your contributions to be genuine and not spurious. If I need sugar as an ingredient in the cake you are assisting me to make, I do not expect you to hand me salt; if I need a spoon, I do not expect a trick spoon made of rubber."[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-Effectiviology-9)

### Maxim of relation (relevance)

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Cooperative_principle&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Maxim of relation (relevance)")]

The maxim of relation is: _be relevant_: the information provided should be relevant to the current exchange and omit any irrelevant information.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-Penn-7)[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-Okanda_et_al-2015-8)[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-Effectiviology-9) In his book, Grice uses the following analogy for this maxim: "I expect a partner's contribution to be appropriate to the immediate needs at each stage of the transaction. If I am mixing ingredients for a cake, I do not expect to be handed a good book, or even an oven cloth (though this might be an appropriate contribution at a later stage)."[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-Effectiviology-9)

With respect to this maxim, Grice writes,

> Though the maxim itself is terse, its formulation conceals a number of problems that exercise me a good deal: questions about what different kinds and focuses of relevance there may be, how these shift in the course of a talk exchange, how to allow for the fact that subjects of conversations are legitimately changed, and so on. I find the treatment of such questions exceedingly difficult, and I hope to revert to them in later work.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-grice-1)

### Maxim of manner (clarity)

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Cooperative_principle&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Maxim of manner (clarity)")]

The maxim of manner is: _be clear_.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-Penn-7)[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-Okanda_et_al-2015-8)[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-Effectiviology-9) Whereas the previous maxims are primarily concerned with _what_ is said, the maxims of manner are concerned with _how_ it is said.[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-Effectiviology-9)

Supermaxim:

*   _Be perspicuous._

Submaxims:[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-Effectiviology-9)

1.   Avoid obscurity of expression — i.e., avoid language that is difficult to understand.
2.   Avoid [ambiguity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambiguity "Ambiguity") — i.e., avoid language that can be interpreted in multiple ways.
3.   Be brief — i.e., avoid unnecessary [verbosity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verbosity "Verbosity").
4.   Be orderly — i.e., provide information in an order that makes sense, and makes it easy for the recipient to process it.[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-Effectiviology-9)

## Maxims in practice

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Cooperative_principle&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Maxims in practice")]

> ...[W]e need first to get clear on the character of Grice's maxims. They are not sociological generalizations about speech, nor they are moral prescriptions or proscriptions on what to say or communicate. Although Grice presented them in the form of guidelines for how to communicate successfully, I think they are better construed as presumptions about utterances, presumptions that we as listeners rely on and as speakers exploit.[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-Top10-11)

Often the addressee of an utterance can add to the overt, surface meaning of a sentence by assuming the speaker has obeyed the maxims. Such additional meanings, if intended by the speaker, are called [conversational implicatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversational_implicature "Conversational implicature"). For example, in the exchange

A (to passer by): I am out of gas.B: There is a gas station round the corner.
A will assume that B obeyed the maxim of relation. However, B's answer is only relevant to A if the gas station is open; so it has the implicature "The gas station is open."[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-grice-1)

Grice did not, however, assume that all people should constantly follow these maxims. Instead, he found it interesting when these were not respected, namely either _flouted_ (with the listener being expected to be able to understand the message) or _violated_ (with the listener being expected to not note this). Flouting means that the circumstances lead us to think that the speaker is nonetheless obeying the cooperative principle, and the maxims are followed on some deeper level, again yielding a conversational implicature. The importance is in what was _not_ said. For example, answering "Are you interested in a game of tennis?" with "It's raining" only disrespects the maxim of relation on the surface; the reasoning behind this utterance is normally clear to the interlocutor.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-grice-1)

### Flouting the maxims

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Cooperative_principle&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Flouting the maxims")]

It is possible to flout a maxim and thereby convey a different meaning than what is literally said.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-grice-1) Often in conversation, a speaker flouts a maxim to produce a negative pragmatic effect, as with [sarcasm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarcasm "Sarcasm") or [irony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irony "Irony"). One can flout the maxim of quality to tell a clumsy friend who has just taken a bad fall that his gracefulness is impressive and obviously mean the complete opposite. Likewise, flouting the maxim of quantity may result in ironic understatement, the maxim of relevance in blame by irrelevant praise, and the maxim of manner in ironic ambiguity.[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-12) The Gricean maxims are therefore often purposefully flouted by [comedians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comedian "Comedian") and writers, who may hide the complete truth and choose their words for the effect of the story and the sake of the reader's experience.[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-13)

Speakers who deliberately flout the maxims usually intend for their listener to understand their underlying implicature. In the case of the clumsy friend, he will most likely understand that the speaker is not truly offering a compliment. Therefore, cooperation is still taking place, but no longer on the literal level. When speakers flout a maxim, they still do so with the aim of expressing some thought. Thus, the Gricean maxims serve a purpose both when they are followed and when they are flouted.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-grice-1)

### Violating the maxims

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Cooperative_principle&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Violating the maxims")]

Similar to [paltering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paltering "Paltering"), violating a maxim means that the speaker is either outright lying by violating the maxim of quality, or being intentionally misleading by violating another maxim. For example, if there was not in fact a gas station around the corner in the example statement above and B was just playing a cruel prank, then B is violating the maxim of quality. A speaker violating the maxim of relevance might imply some fact is important when it is not; warning a cook that it takes a considerable length of time to heat the oven implies that preheating the oven is helpful and should be done, but perhaps the speaker knows the recipe does not actually involve baking anything. Violating the maxim of quantity can involve intentionally including useless details in an attempt to obscure or distract, or via telling half-truths that leave off important details such as the gas station being abandoned and not in operation anymore.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-grice-1)

## Criticism

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Cooperative_principle&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Criticism")]

Grice's theory is often disputed by arguing that cooperative conversation, like most [social behaviour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_behavior "Social behavior"), is [culturally determined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_determinism "Cultural determinism"), and therefore the Gricean maxims and the cooperative principle do not universally apply because of [cultural differences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_differences "Cultural differences"). Keenan (1976) claims, for example, that the [Malagasy people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malagasy_people "Malagasy people") follow a completely opposite cooperative principle to achieve conversational cooperation. In their culture, speakers are reluctant to share information and flout the maxim of quantity by evading direct questions and relying on incomplete answers because of the risk of losing face by committing oneself to the truth of the information, as well as the fact that having information is a form of [prestige](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prestige_(sociolinguistics) "Prestige (sociolinguistics)").[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-14)[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-15) To push back on this point, Harnish (1976)[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-16) points out that Grice only claims his maxims hold in conversations where the cooperative principle is in effect. The Malagasy speakers choose not to be cooperative, valuing the prestige of information ownership more highly. (It could also be said in this case that this is a less cooperative communication system, since less information is shared.)

Some argue that the maxims are vague.[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-17) This may explain the criticism that the Gricean maxims can easily be misinterpreted to be a guideline for [etiquette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etiquette "Etiquette"), instructing speakers on how to be [moral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moral "Moral"), [polite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politeness "Politeness") conversationalists. However, the Gricean maxims, despite their wording, are only meant to describe the commonly accepted traits of successful cooperative communication.[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-Top10-11)[Geoffrey Leech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Leech "Geoffrey Leech") introduced the [politeness maxims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politeness_maxims "Politeness maxims"): tact, [generosity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generosity "Generosity"), approbation, modesty, [agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consensus_decision-making "Consensus decision-making"), and [sympathy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sympathy "Sympathy").

It has also been noted by [relevance theorists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relevance_theory "Relevance theory") that [conversational implicatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversational_implicature "Conversational implicature") can arise in uncooperative situations, which cannot be accounted for in Grice's framework. For example, assume that A and B are planning a holiday in France and A suggests they visit their old acquaintance Gérard; and further, that B knows where Gérard lives, and A knows that B knows. The following dialogue ensues:

A: Where does Gérard live?B: Somewhere in the South of France.
This is understood by A as B not wanting to say where exactly Gérard lives, precisely _because_ B is not following the cooperative principle.[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#cite_note-18)
