In sociology, **face** refers to a class of behaviors and customs, associated with the [morality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morality "Morality"), [honor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honour "Honour"), and [authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authority "Authority") of an individual (or group of individuals), and their image within [social](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social "Social") groups. Face is linked to the [dignity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dignity "Dignity") and [prestige](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reputation "Reputation") that a person enjoys in terms of their [social relationships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_relationships "Social relationships"). This idea, with varying nuances, is observed in many societies and cultures, including [Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_people "Chinese people"), [Arab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_world "Arab world"), [Indonesian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesia "Indonesia"), [Korean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korea "Korea"), [Malaysian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysia "Malaysia"), [Laotian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laos "Laos"), [Indian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India "India"), [Japanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan "Japan"), [Vietnamese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam "Vietnam"), [Filipino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippines "Philippines"), [Thai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thailand "Thailand"), [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Iran "Culture of Iran"), [Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia "Russia"), and other [East Slavic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Slavs "East Slavs") cultures.

The usage of "face" in the [English language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language "English language") is borrowed from [Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_language "Chinese language").[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-1) Face has particularly complex dynamics and meanings within the context of [Chinese culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_culture "Chinese culture") and is closely associated with [Confucianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confucianism "Confucianism"). The Chinese concept of face has also heavily influenced the cultures of [Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan "Japan"), [Vietnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam "Vietnam") and [Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korea "Korea").[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-2)

## Definitions

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Face_(sociological_concept)&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Definitions")]

Although Chinese writer [Lin Yutang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lin_Yutang "Lin Yutang") claimed "face cannot be translated or defined",[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-Yutang-3) these definitions have been created:

*   Face is an image of [self](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self "Self") delineated in terms of approved social attributes.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation\_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
*   Face is the respectability and/or deference which a person can claim for themself or from others.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation\_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
*   Face is a quality that can be lost, maintained, or enhanced, and must be constantly attended to in interaction.[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-4)
*   Face is a sense of worth that comes from knowing one's [status](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_status "Social status") and reflecting concern with the congruence between one's [performance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Performance "Performance") or appearance and one's real worth.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation\_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
*   "Face" means "sociodynamic valuation", a [lexical hyponym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyponymy_and_hypernymy "Hyponymy and hypernymy") of words meaning "prestige; dignity; honor; respect; status".[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation\_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

## By culture

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Face_(sociological_concept)&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: By culture")]

### Arab world

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Face_(sociological_concept)&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Arab world")]

In [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic "Arabic"), the expression _hafiẓa māʼ al-wajh_ ([حفظ ماء الوجه](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%D8%AD%D9%81%D8%B8%20%D9%85%D8%A7%D8%A1%20%D8%A7%D9%84%D9%88%D8%AC%D9%87 "wikt:حفظ ماء الوجه"), lit.'save the face's water', is used to mean _save face_. The entire [Arab culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_culture "Arab culture") of social and family behavior is based around [Islamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam "Islam") concepts of dignity. For Shia Islam, face is based on the social and family ranking system found in the Treatise of Rights, [Al-Risalah al-Huquq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Risalah_al-Huquq "Al-Risalah al-Huquq"), Shia Islam's primary source for social behaviors.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-5)

### East Asia

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Face_(sociological_concept)&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: East Asia")]

#### Chinese

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Face_(sociological_concept)&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Chinese")]

In China, in particular, the concepts of _mianzi_, _[lian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lian\_(Chinese\_social\_concept)?action=edit&redlink=1 "Lian (Chinese social concept) (page does not exist)")_ and _[yan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yan\_(Chinese\_social\_concept)?action=edit&redlink=1 "Yan (Chinese social concept) (page does not exist)")_ play an extremely important role in the fabric of society.

In [Chinese culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_culture "Chinese culture"), "face" refers to two distinct concepts, although linked in Chinese social relations. One is _mianzi_ (面子), and the other is _lian_ (臉), which are used regularly in everyday language although not so much in formal writing.

Two influential Chinese authors explained face. The Chinese writer [Lu Xun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lu_Xun "Lu Xun")[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-6) referred to the American missionary [Arthur Henderson Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Henderson_Smith "Arthur Henderson Smith")'s interpretation.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-7)

> The term "face" keeps cropping up in our conversation, and it seems such a simple expression that I doubt whether many people give it much thought. Recently, however, we have heard this word on the lips of foreigners too, who seem to be studying it. They find it extremely hard to understand, but believe that "face" is the key to the Chinese spirit and that grasping it will be like grabbing a queue twenty-four years ago [when wearing a [queue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queue_(hairstyle) "Queue (hairstyle)") was compulsory]– everything else will follow.[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-8)[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-9)

[Lin Yutang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lin_Yutang "Lin Yutang") considered the psychology of "face":

> Interesting as the Chinese physiological face is, the psychological face makes a still more fascinating study. It is not a face that can be washed or shaved, but a face that can be "granted" and "lost" and "fought for" and "presented as a gift". Here we arrive at the most curious point of Chinese social psychology. Abstract and intangible, it is yet the most delicate standard by which Chinese social intercourse is regulated.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-Yutang-3)

The concept of face has a significant role in [Chinese diplomacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_relations_of_China "Foreign relations of China").[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-:102-10):123

**_Miàn_** (面) "face; [personal esteem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-esteem "Self-esteem"); countenance; surface; side" occurs in words like:

*   _miànzi_ ([面子](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E9%9D%A2%E5%AD%90 "wikt:面子")) "face; side; reputation; self-respect; prestige, honor; social standing."[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation\_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_] It is similar to the concept of "keeping up with appearances".[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-11)
*   _miànmù_ ([面目](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E9%9D%A2%E7%9B%AE "wikt:面目"); 'face and eyes') "face; appearance; respect; social standing; prestige; honor (only used in ancient [Chinese prose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_prose "Chinese prose"). Now it only means [appearance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_appearance "Visual appearance"))"
*   _miànpí_ (面皮; 'face skin') "facial skin; complexion; feelings; sensitivity; [sense of shame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shame "Shame")"
*   _tǐmiàn_ ([體面](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E9%AB%94%E9%9D%A2 "wikt:體面"); 'body face') "face; good looking; honor; dignity; prestige"
*   _qíngmian_ (情面; 'feelings face') "face; prestige; favor; kindness; partiality"

Hsien-chin Hu says “face”

> can be borrowed, struggled for, added to, padded, — all terms indicating a gradual increase in volume. It is built up through initial high position, wealth, power, ability, through cleverly establishing social ties to a number of prominent people, as well as through avoidance of acts that would cause unfavorable comment.[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-hu44-12):61

**_Liǎn_** (臉) "face; countenance; respect; reputation; prestige" is seen in several face words:

*   _liǎnshàng_ (臉上; 'face on/above') "one's face; honor; respect"
*   _liǎnmiàn_ (臉面; 'face face') "face; self-respect; prestige; influence"
*   _liǎnpí_ (臉皮; 'face skin') "face; sensitivity; compassion"

Hu contrasts _méiyǒu liǎn_ (沒有臉; 'without face') "audacious; wanton; shameless" as "the most severe condemnation that can be made of a person" and _bùyào liǎn_ (不要臉; 'don't want face') "shameless; selfishly inconsiderate" as "a serious accusation meaning that ego does not care what society thinks of his character, that he is ready to obtain benefits for himself in defiance of [moral standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morality "Morality")".[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-hu44-12):51–52

**_Yán_** (顏) "face; prestige; reputation; honor" occurs in the common expression _diū yán_ 丟顏 and the words:

*   _yánhòu_ (顏厚; 'face thick') or _hòuyán_ 厚顏 "thick-skinned; brazen; shameless; impudent"
*   _yánmiàn_ (顏面; 'face face') "face; honor; prestige"

#### Japanese

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Face_(sociological_concept)&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Japanese")]

In Japan, the concept of face is known as _mentsu_ (面子), which is defined as “the public image people want to present within a given social framework”.[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-:0-13) More specifically, mentsu can only be established when in social situations where others are present. It is associated with the fulfillment of one's social role(s) as expected by others.[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-14) There are two main types of face in Japanese culture:

*   _Menboku_ (面目) refers to “aspects of the self approved of or respectability given by others”. This encompasses the fulfillment of one's duty in social settings.[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-:1-15)
*   _Taimen_ (体面) refers to the projected self or ostentation, which involves “the duty to clear one’s reputation of insult or imputation of failure”.[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-:1-15)

The terms _menboku_ and _taimen_ were borrowed into Japanese from the Chinese _miànmù_ and _tǐmiàn_ during the [Heian period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heian_period "Heian period"), while _mentsu_, from _miànzi_, entered the language in the 1920s–1930s.[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-16)

The need for positive self-regard is culturally variant and Japanese motivations for positive self-regard differ from those of other cultures in that it is primarily self-critically focused.[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-:2-17) From a young age, children are encouraged by parents to become socially shared images of the ideal person through the phrase “_rashii_” (らしい；similar to).[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-:2-17) In this way, social roles influence how Japanese identify themselves but also establish the desirable image Japanese people wish to present in front of others.[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-:3-18) “Japanese competition characterized by _yokonarabi_ (横並び), emphasizing not on surpassing others, but on not falling behind others”.[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-:2-17) The continual effort to improve oneself as summarized by the saying _gambarimasu_ (頑張ります) can be viewed as an expression to secure the esteem of others, illustrating high motivations to maintain public face in Japanese culture.[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-19)

In contrast to the Chinese notion of _mianzi_ which emphasizes one's power, the Japanese notion of _mentsu_ places emphasis on social roles.[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-:0-13) A comparative study of Japanese and Chinese student's perceptions of face revealed that Japanese students tend to be more concerned about face in situations relating to social status and appropriate treatment of others based on social status, while Chinese students tend to be more concerned in situations concerning evaluations of competence or performance.[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-:0-13)

The integration of face in Japanese culture is evident in the language and cultural norms. According to Matsumoto 1988, “To attend to each other’s face in Japanese culture is to recognize each other’s social position and to convey such a recognition through the proper linguistic means, including formulaic expressions, honorifics, verbs of giving and receiving, and other “relation-acknowledging devices”. The Japanese cultural norms of [_honne_ (本音; inner feelings) and _tatemae_ (建前; presented stance)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honne_and_tatemae "Honne and tatemae"),[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-:4-20) a commonly understood model of communication whereby individuals put up a polite “front” that hides their real beliefs,[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-:2-17) emphasize the importance placed on carrying out social responsibility in Japanese society.[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-:4-20)

In the politeness-orientated Japanese society, simple sentences in English would have many variations in Japanese where the speaker must make linguistic choices based on their [interpersonal relationship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interpersonal_relationship "Interpersonal relationship") with the listener.[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-:5-21) Common greetings in Japanese such as _yoroshiku_ _onegaishimasu_ (よろしくお願いします; I make a request and I hope things go well) highlight the debt-sensitive culture in Japan.[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-:6-22) By emphasizing the speaker's debt to giving credit to the listener, one implies the debt will be repaid, this is rooted in the Japanese concept of face.[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-:6-22) In addition, phrases such as _sumimasen_ (すみません), originally an expression for apology but encompasses feelings of both gratitude and apology, are used across a variety of contexts, highlighting the use of language to maintain and reinforce smooth face-to-face interactions within Japanese society.[[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-23)

A study investigating the conditions that led to feelings of face-loss in Japanese participants revealed that the presence of others and engagement in activities related to social roles led to a stronger face-loss experience.[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-:3-18) When examining _mentsu_ in Japan, it was revealed that people generally regard experiences of losing one's own face as unpleasant. Experiences of face-saving and face-loss can influence one's mood and self-esteem.[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-:7-24) Moreover, people's moods can be influenced by whether the face of those close to them are saved.[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-:7-24) Findings also reveal that caring for others through saving face can have a positive impact on one's interpersonal relationships with others.[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-:7-24)

### Persian cultural sphere

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Face_(sociological_concept)&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Persian cultural sphere")]

In [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_language "Persian language"), expressions like "_Aab ro rizi_" (آبروريزی, lit.'losing the face's water'), is used to mean _save face_ and "_Dou roi_" (دورويی, lit.'two-facedness'), "_Ro seyahi_" (_nq_, lit.'Black-facedness') meaning "ashamed and embarrassed" and "_Ro sepidi_" (روسپيدی, lit.'white-facedness') meaning "proud" (opposite of _Ro seyahi_) are used. So Persian speakers use some strategies in saving the face or character of each other while they communicate.

### Southeast Asia

#### Burmese (Myanmar)

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Face_(sociological_concept)&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Burmese (Myanmar)")]

The concept of "face" is important in Burmese society.[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-26) The [Burmese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burmese_language "Burmese language") word for face is _myet-hna_ (မျက်နှာ), and is used in many compound words relating to the concept of "face."[[27]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-27) The doublets _myet-hna pyet_ (မျက်နှာပျက်) and _myet-hna phyet_ (မျက်နှာဖျက်) mean to "lose face" and "to cause the loss of face" respectively. The loss of face is associated with an inability to control one's feelings and lose composure, especially in public.[[28]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-28)

#### Vietnamese

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Face_(sociological_concept)&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: Vietnamese")]

The [Vietnamese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_language "Vietnamese language") phrase for saving face is called giữ thế diện or giữ mặ. As Vietnam is classified under the [Sinosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinosphere "Sinosphere"), the Chinese concept of "face" has been introduced to Vietnam during the eras of [Bắc thuộc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam_under_Chinese_rule "Vietnam under Chinese rule").[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-29)

#### Khmer (Cambodian)

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Face_(sociological_concept)&action=edit&section=12 "Edit section: Khmer (Cambodian)")]

The [Khmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khmer_language "Khmer language") word for face is _muk_ (មុខ, lit.'face'). _Bat muk_ (បាត់មុខ) translates literally as 'lose face'. _Tuk muk_ (ទុកមុខ) translates literally as 'save face' or 'preserve face'. This concept is understood and treated much the same in Cambodia as elsewhere in Asia.

#### Thai

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Face_(sociological_concept)&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section: Thai")]

The [Thai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thai_language "Thai language") word for face is _naa_ (หน้า, lit.'face'). There are basically two main ways of expressing loss of face: One, _sia naa_ (เสียหน้า), translates literally as 'lose face.' Another term, _khai naa_ (ขายหน้า) means 'sale of face'. The actual connotation of _khai naa_ is that the person who lost face did so through fault of self or through the thoughtless action of another. As in China and other regions where loss of face is important, the Thai version involves sociodynamic status.

### Western world

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Face_(sociological_concept)&action=edit&section=14 "Edit section: Western world")]

#### English

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Face_(sociological_concept)&action=edit&section=15 "Edit section: English")]

> The English [semantic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantic_field "Semantic field") for "face" words meaning "prestige; honor" is smaller than the corresponding Chinese field. English face meaning "prestige; honor, respect, dignity, status, reputation, social acceptance, or good name. The "lose" verb in "lose face" means "fail to maintain", while the "save" in "save face" means "avoid loss/damage". The country begins to feel that Government consented to arrangements by which China has lost face; the officials have long been conscious that they are becoming ridiculous in the eyes of the people, seeing that where a foreigner is concerned they can neither enforce a Chinese right, nor redress a Chinese grievance, even on Chinese soil.[[30]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-30)

Several American newspapers from 1874 listed the concept in a column of "Chinese Proverbs" or "Facts & Fancies" stating "The Chinese, be it observed, are great sticklers for propriety and respectability, and are very much afraid of what they term 'losing face'."[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-31)[[32]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-32)_Loss of face_ occurs in _[The Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The\_Times "The Times")_ (August 3, 1929): "Each wishes to concede only what can be conceded without loss of 'face'".[[33]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-33)

_[Save face](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/save%20face "wikt:save face")_ was coined from _lose face_ applying the semantic [opposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opposite_(semantics) "Opposite (semantics)") between _[lose](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/lose "wikt:lose")_ and _[save](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/save "wikt:save")_ ([Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplified_Chinese_characters "Simplified Chinese characters"):保面子; [pinyin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinyin "Pinyin"):_bǎo miànzi_; lit.'guard/save face'; when successful, it's called 保住面子; _bǎozhu miànzi_; 'saved/guarded face').

The _[Oxford English Dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford\_English\_Dictionary "Oxford English Dictionary")_ (OED) defines "save face" as: "To keep, protect or guard (a thing) from damage, loss, or destruction", and elaborates,

> 8f. to save one's face: to avoid being disgraced or humiliated. Similarly, to save (another's) face. Hence save-face adj. = _face-saving_ ... Originally used by the English community in China, with reference to the continual devices among the Chinese to avoid incurring or inflicting disgrace. The exact phrase appears not to occur in Chinese, but ‘to lose face’ (_diu lien_), and ‘for the sake of his face’, are common.[[34]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-34)

Among the [English words of Chinese origin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_English_words_of_Chinese_origin "List of English words of Chinese origin"), _lose face_ is an uncommon [verb phrase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verb_phrase "Verb phrase") and a unique [semantic loan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantic_loan "Semantic loan") translation. Most Anglo-Chinese borrowings are [nouns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noun "Noun"),[[35]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-35):250 with a few exceptions such as _[to kowtow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kowtow "Kowtow")_, _[to Shanghai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shanghaiing "Shanghaiing")_, _[to brainwash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brainwashing "Brainwashing")_, and _lose face_. English _face_, meaning "prestige" or "honor", is the only case of a Chinese [semantic loan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantic_loan "Semantic loan"). Semantic loans extend an indigenous word's meaning in conformity with a foreign model (e.g., the French _realiser_, lit.'achieve'or 'create'or 'construct', used in the sense of English _realize_). The vast majority of English words from Chinese are ordinary [loanwords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loanwords "Loanwords") with regular phonemic adaptation (e.g., _[chop suey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chop\_suey "Chop suey")_< Cantonese _tsap-sui_[雜碎](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E9%9B%9C%E7%A2%8E "wikt:雜碎")lit.'miscellaneous pieces'). A few are [calques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calque "Calque") where a borrowing is blended with native elements (e.g., _chopsticks_< Pidgin _chop_ "quick, fast" < Cantonese _kap_[急](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E6%80%A5 "wikt:急")lit.'quick' + _stick_). _Face_ meaning "prestige" is technically a loan synonym, owing to semantic overlap between the native English meaning "outward semblance; [effrontery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effrontery "Effrontery")" and the borrowed Chinese meaning "prestige; dignity".

When _face_ acquired its Chinese sense of "prestige; honor", it filled a [lexical gap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lexical_gap "Lexical gap") in the English [lexicon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lexicon "Lexicon"). Chan and Kwok write,

> The Chinese has supplied a specific "name" for a "thing" embodying qualities not expressed or possibly not fully expressed, by a number of terms in English. The aptness of the figurative extension has probably also played a part[[36]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-36)

Carr concludes,

> The nearest English synonyms of the apt figurative _face_ are _prestige_, _honor_, _respect_, _dignity_, _status_, _reputation_, _social acceptance_, or _good name_.[[37]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-carr-37)[[38]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-ho-38):847–880[[39]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-39) explains how "face" is a more basic meaning than "status", "dignity", or "honor". "Prestige" appears to be semantically closest to "face", however a person can be said to have _face_ but not _prestige_, or vice versa. Prestige is not necessary; one can easily live without it, but hardly without "face".[[37]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-carr-37)

#### Russian

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Face_(sociological_concept)&action=edit&section=16 "Edit section: Russian")]

Russian Orthodox concept of face (лик, лицо, личина) is different from the Chinese concept of face in regards to different emphasis on sanctity and individualism, and in regards to different understanding of the opposites. However, both Russian and Chinese concepts of "face" are close to each other in their focus on person being, first and foremost, part of larger community. In contrast to co-existence of personal individualism with their simultaneous participation in community affairs within Western culture, individuality is much more toned-down in both Russian and Chinese cultures in favour of communality; both Russian and Chinese cultures are lacking in stark Western [dichotomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dichotomy "Dichotomy") of "internal" vs. "external", and also lacking in Western focus on legal frameworks being foundation for individualism; and instead of it, in both Russian and Chinese cultures ritualism in public relations is much more highly regarded than in Western culture, where in the West ritualism is thought of to be mostly dull and empty of content.[[40]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-ProsekovS-40)

The importance of the concept of face in Russia may be seen imprinted into amassment of proverbs and sayings, where the word лицо is used as a reference to one's character or reputation, for instance упасть в грязь лицом (lit.'to fall face down into mud') meaning "to lose reputation", двуличие (lit.'two-facedness'or 'the absence of a well-defined face') denoting a negative trait, потерять лицо, similarly to упасть в грязь лицом, but stronger, meaning to "lose reputation or social standing", and лик meaning both "face" and at the same time "the essence", when being used to describe a person, showing that there is high expectation of "inner self" and "outer self" of a person being in high accord with each other, looking from the framework of Russian culture. Having a лик is opposed to wearing a личина, meaning 'a mask' or 'facade,' which carries a strong negative connotation in Russian culture.

#### South Slavic

Among [South Slavs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Slavs "South Slavs"), especially in [Serbo-Croatian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serbo-Croatian "Serbo-Croatian") and [Bulgarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulgarian_language "Bulgarian language"), the word _obraz_ (образ) is used as a traditional expression for [honor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honor "Honor") and the sociological concept of face. ## Academic interpretations

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Face_(sociological_concept)&action=edit&section=18 "Edit section: Academic interpretations")]

### Sociology

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Face_(sociological_concept)&action=edit&section=19 "Edit section: Sociology")]

"Face" is central to [sociology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociology "Sociology") and [sociolinguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociolinguistics "Sociolinguistics"). Martin C. Yang[[42]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-42) analyzed eight sociological factors in [losing or gaining face](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_negotiation_theory "Face negotiation theory"): the kinds of equality between the people involved, their ages, personal sensibilities, inequality in social status, social relationship, consciousness of personal prestige, presence of a witness, and the particular social value/sanction involved.[[43]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-43)

The sociologist [Erving Goffman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erving_Goffman "Erving Goffman") introduced the concept of "face" into [social theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_theory "Social theory") with his 1955 article "On Face-work: An Analysis of Ritual Elements of Social Interaction" and 1967 book _Interaction Ritual: Essays on Face-to-Face Behavior_.[[44]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-44)[[45]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-45) According to Goffman's [dramaturgical perspective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dramaturgical_perspective "Dramaturgical perspective"), face is a [mask](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mask "Mask") that changes depending on the [audience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audience "Audience") and the variety of social interaction. People strive to maintain the face they have created in social situations. They are emotionally attached to their faces, so they feel good when their faces are maintained; loss of face results in emotional pain, so in [social interactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_interaction "Social interaction") people [cooperate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperation "Cooperation") by using [politeness strategies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politeness_theory "Politeness theory") to maintain each other's faces.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation\_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

Face is sociologically universal. People "are human", [Joseph Agassi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Agassi "Joseph Agassi") and I. C. Jarvie believe, "because they have face to care for – without it they lose human dignity."[[46]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-46):140 Hu elaborates:

> The point is that face is distinctively human. Anyone who does not wish to declare his social bankruptcy must show a regard for face: he must claim for himself, and must extend to others, some degree of compliance, respect, and deference in order to maintain a minimum level of effective social functioning. While it is true that the conceptualization of what constitutes face and the rules governing face behavior vary considerably across cultures, the concern for face is invariant. Defined at a high level of generality, the concept of face is a universal.[[38]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-ho-38):881–882

The sociological concept of face has recently been reanalyzed through consideration of the Chinese concepts of face (_mianzi_ and _lian_) which permits deeper understanding of the various dimensions of experience of face, including moral and social evaluation, and its emotional mechanisms.[[47]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-47)

### Marketing

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Face_(sociological_concept)&action=edit&section=20 "Edit section: Marketing")]

According to Hu, mianzi stands for "the kind of prestige that is emphasized...a reputation achieved through getting on in life, through success and ostentation", while face is "the respect of a group for a man with a good moral reputation: the man who will fulfill his obligations regardless of the hardships involved, who under all circumstances shows himself a decent human being".[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-hu44-12) The concept seems to relate to two different meanings, from one side Chinese consumers try to increase or maintain their reputation (_mianzi_) in front of socially and culturally significant others (e.g. friends); on the other hand, they try to defend or save face.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation\_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

_Mianzi_ is not only important to improve the consumer's reputation in front of significant others, but rather it is also associated with feelings of dignity, honor, and pride.[[48]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-Filieri_et_al_2018-48) In [consumer behaviour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consumer_behaviour "Consumer behaviour") literature, _mianzi_ has been used to explain Chinese consumer purchasing behaviour and brand choice[[49]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-Filieri_&_Lin_2017-49) and considered it as a quality owned by some brands. Some consumers tend to favour some brands (and their products and services) because of their capacity to enable them to gain _mianzi_, which does not mean simply increase their reputation but also to show achievements and communicate these achievements to others in order to be more accepted in social circles, especially upper class circles.[[50]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-Filieri_et_al_2017-50) Chinese consumers tend to believe that if they buy some brands it is easier to be accepted in the social circles of powerful and wealthy people. Connections are particularly important in Chinese culture as people use social connections to achieve their goals.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation\_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

However, _mianzi_ has also an emotional facet.[[50]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-Filieri_et_al_2017-50) Consumers feel proud, special, honoured, even more valuable as individuals if they can afford to buy brands that can enhance their _mianzi_. Therefore, some branded products and services, especially those that require conspicuous consumption (e.g. smartphones, bags, shoes), are chosen because they foster feelings of pride and vanity in the owner.[[49]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-Filieri_&_Lin_2017-49)[[50]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-Filieri_et_al_2017-50)

A brand that enables an individual to achieve such goals in life, in branding literature, it is labelled as 'brand mianzi', which is the capacity of a brand to provide emotions and self-enhancement to its owner.[[49]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-Filieri_&_Lin_2017-49)[[50]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-Filieri_et_al_2017-50)

Scholars have proved that brand _mianzi_ affects consumer purchase intentions[[49]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-Filieri_&_Lin_2017-49)[[50]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-Filieri_et_al_2017-50) and brand equity.[[48]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-Filieri_et_al_2018-48)

In summary, _mianzi_ is a cultural concept that relates to the social, emotional and psychological dimension of consumption and has an impact on consumers’ perception of their self and purchase decisions. Purchase and consumption of brands (but also other activities, like choosing a specific university), in Chinese culture, are profoundly affected by _mianzi_ and different brands can be more or less apt to enhance or maintain _mianzi_, while others can cause a loss of face.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation\_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

### Politeness theory

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Face_(sociological_concept)&action=edit&section=21 "Edit section: Politeness theory")]

[Penelope Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penelope_Brown "Penelope Brown") and [Stephen C. Levinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_C._Levinson "Stephen C. Levinson") (1987) expanded Goffman's [theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory "Theory") of face in their [politeness theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politeness_theory "Politeness theory"), which differentiated between [positive and negative face](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politeness_theory#Positive_and_negative_face "Politeness theory") (p.61).[[51]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-:8-51)[[52]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-miller-52)[[53]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-53)[[54]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-54)

*   **Positive face** is "the positive consistent [self-image](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self_image "Self image") or '[personality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personality_type "Personality type")' (crucially including the [desire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desire_(emotion) "Desire (emotion)") that this self-image be appreciated and approved of) claimed by interactants"
*   **Negative face** is "the basic claim to territories, personal preserves, [rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rights "Rights") to non-[distraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distraction "Distraction")—i.e., to freedom of action and freedom from imposition"

In human interactions, people are often forced to threaten either an addressee's positive and/or negative face, and so there are various politeness strategies to mitigate those face-threatening acts.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation\_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

However, researchers disagree on the universality of Politeness Theory, arguing it fails to consider the cultural origins of the face and behaviors in non-western cultures where interactions focus on group identity rather than individuality.[[55]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-:9-55)

For instance, the Chinese origins of “face” was not considered by Brown and Levinson.[[55]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-:9-55) Concerning the concept of negative face, obtaining _mianzi_ in Chinese culture results in the recognition of one's claim to respect from the community, not freedom of action.[[55]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-:9-55) Japanese researcher claims the concept of negative face is alien to Japanese culture, and mistakenly assume the basic unit of society is the individual which is incongruent with the importance placed on interpersonal relationships in Japanese culture.[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-:5-21) In the case of Japan, individuals obtain face to maintain one's position in relation to other members of the same community.[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-:5-21)

These differences suggest the concept of face according to the Politeness Theory is centered around the ideal individual autonomy. However, the concept of face in Eastern cultures such as the Chinese and Japanese orientate towards social identity.[[55]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-:9-55)

### Communication theory

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Face_(sociological_concept)&action=edit&section=22 "Edit section: Communication theory")]

Tae-Seop Lim and John Waite Bowers (1991) claim that face is the public image that a person claims for himself. Within this claim there are three dimensions. "_Autonomy face_" describes a desire to appear independent, in control, and responsible. "_Fellowship face_" describes a desire to seem cooperative, accepted, and loved. "_Competence face_" describes a desire to appear intelligent, accomplished, and capable.[[56]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-56)[[52]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-miller-52)[_[page needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing\_sources "Wikipedia:Citing sources")_] Oetzel et al. (2000) defined "facework" as "the communicative strategies one uses to enact self-face and to uphold, support, or challenge another person's face". In terms of [interpersonal communication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interpersonal_communication "Interpersonal communication"), Facework refers to an individual's [identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_identity "Personal identity") in a [social world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_reality "Social reality") and how that identity is created, reinforced, diminished, and maintained in [communicative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communication "Communication") interactions.[[57]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-57)

### Facework

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Face_(sociological_concept)&action=edit&section=23 "Edit section: Facework")]

**Facework**[[58]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-Fletcher-58) represents the transition from the [real self](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_self "Real self") of the [individual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Individual "Individual") to the image he or she represents to society for [communicative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communicative_competence "Communicative competence") or [leadership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leadership "Leadership") purposes. This concept is all about presentation of the dignified [image](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image "Image") which soon will become as an [authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authority "Authority") for other individuals. Facework is a skill of constantly maintaining the face in order to deserve the respect and honor from it. For instance, [individualistic cultures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Individualistic_culture "Individualistic culture") like [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States "United States"), [Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canada "Canada"), and [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany "Germany") are standing for the position of protecting the self-face of the individual while [collectivist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collectivism_and_individualism "Collectivism and individualism") cultures such as [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China "China"), [South Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Korea "South Korea"), and [Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan "Japan") support the idea of maintaining the other-face for self-dignity and self-respect.

There are also exist other facework strategies not always basing on the culture strategies like [face-negotiating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_negotiation_theory "Face negotiation theory"),[[59]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-59) face-constituting, face-compensating, face-honoring, face-saving, face-threatening, face-building, face-protecting, face-depreciating, face-giving, face-restoring, and face-neutral.[[58]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-Fletcher-58)

### Intercultural communication

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Face_(sociological_concept)&action=edit&section=24 "Edit section: Intercultural communication")]

Face is central to [intercultural communication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intercultural_communication "Intercultural communication") or [cross-cultural communication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-cultural_communication "Cross-cultural communication"). [Bert Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bert_Brown "Bert Brown") explains the importance of both personal and national face in international negotiations:

> Among the most troublesome kinds of problems that arise in negotiation are the intangible issues related to loss of face. In some instances, protecting against loss of face becomes so central an issue that it swamps the importance of the tangible issues at stake and generates intense conflicts that can impede progress toward agreement and increase substantially the costs of conflict resolution.[[60]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-60)

In terms of [Edward T. Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_T._Hall "Edward T. Hall")'s dichotomy between [high context cultures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_context_culture "High context culture") focused upon in-groups and [low context cultures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_context_culture "Low context culture") focused upon individuals, face-saving is generally viewed as more important in high context cultures such as China or Japan than in low-context ones such as the United States or Germany.[[61]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-face-saving-61)

### Face-negotiation theory

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Face_(sociological_concept)&action=edit&section=25 "Edit section: Face-negotiation theory")]

Stella Ting-Toomey developed [Face Negotiation Theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_negotiation_theory "Face negotiation theory") to explain cultural differences in communication and conflict resolution. Ting-Toomey defines face as:

> [...] the interaction between the degree of threats or considerations one party offers to another party, and the degree of claim for a sense of self-respect (or demand for respect toward one's national image or cultural group) put forth by the other party in a given situation.[[62]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-62)

### Psychology

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Face_(sociological_concept)&action=edit&section=26 "Edit section: Psychology")]

The [psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology "Psychology") of "face" is another field of research. [Wolfram Eberhard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfram_Eberhard "Wolfram Eberhard"), who analyzed Chinese "[guilt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guilt_(emotion) "Guilt (emotion)")" and "[sin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sin "Sin")" in terms of literary psychology, debunked the persistent myth that "face" is peculiar to the Chinese rather than a force in every human society. Eberhard noted

> It is mainly in the writings of foreigners that we find the stress upon shame in Chinese society; it is they who stated that the Chinese were typically afraid of "losing their face". It is they who reported many cases of suicide because of loss of face, or of suicide in order to punish another person after one's death as a ghost, or to cause through suicide endless difficulties or even punishment to the other person. But in the Chinese literature used here, including also the short stories, I did not once find the phrase "losing face"; and there was no clear case of suicide because of shame alone.[[63]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-63)

The [Chinese University of Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_University_of_Hong_Kong "Chinese University of Hong Kong") social psychologist Michael Harris Bond observed that in Hong Kong,

> Given the importance of having face and of being related to those who do, there is a plethora of relationship politics in Chinese culture. Name dropping, eagerness to associate with the rich and famous, the use of external status symbols, sensitivity to insult, lavish gift-giving, the use of titles, the sedulous avoidance of criticism, all abound, and require considerable readjustment for someone used to organizing social life by impersonal rules, frankness, and greater equality.[[64]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-64)

### Political science

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Face_(sociological_concept)&action=edit&section=27 "Edit section: Political science")]

"Face" has further applications in [political science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_science "Political science"). For instance, [Susan Pharr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susan_Pharr "Susan Pharr") stressed the importance of "losing face" in Japanese [comparative politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_politics "Comparative politics").[[65]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-65)

### Semantics

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Face_(sociological_concept)&action=edit&section=28 "Edit section: Semantics")]

Linguists have analyzed the [semantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantics "Semantics") of "face". Huang used [prototype semantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prototype_semantics "Prototype semantics") to differentiate _lian_ and _mianzi_.[[66]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-66)[George Lakoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Lakoff "George Lakoff") and [Mark Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Johnson_(philosopher) "Mark Johnson (philosopher)")'s _[Metaphors We Live By](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphors\_We\_Live\_By "Metaphors We Live By")_ emphasizes "the face for the person" [metonymy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metonymy "Metonymy").[[67]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-67):37[Keith Allan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keith_Allan_(linguist) "Keith Allan (linguist)") (1986) extended "face" into theoretical semantics. He postulated it to be an essential element of all language interchanges, and claimed: "A satisfactory theory of linguistic meaning cannot ignore questions of face presentation, nor other politeness phenomena that maintain the co-operative nature of language interchange."[[68]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_(sociological_concept)#cite_note-68)
